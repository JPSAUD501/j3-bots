# Experience

This document describes what the operator does and sees. It is written from their side: the question each screen answers comes before the screen.

## Flows

### First run

The operator chooses where the bots live. In the desktop app, the first screen asks: "Use this computer" runs them on that computer, where they sleep when it sleeps, and goes straight on with nothing to type, while J3 Bots starts there; "Connect to a server" leads to the address of a machine that stays on, where the operator installed J3 Bots, and pairing with it. A browser or a phone always pairs with a server. Until the first bot exists, the app is a short the operator plays: one stage, a few bots on it, and one thing said or asked at a time under them. It says what J3 Bots is before it asks anything ([decision 0065](../decisions/0065-the-first-run-tells-what-j3-bots-is-before-it-asks.md)): three steps the stage tells, each one idea in a few words, then the steps that make the first bot. Every step can be left with its defaults: Continue always works, at once, wherever the stage is in what it is doing, and Back, the screen's or the phone's own, always goes to the step before. Dots at the top say how many steps there are and which this is. Left mid-way, by a closed tab or an app put away, it starts again where it was, with the name typed and the look picked.

Under every step one quiet line says what the server is still getting ready, when it is: "Preparing your computer…" in the desktop app running the bots on its own computer, "Preparing your server…" elsewhere, with how far the downloads are when the server knows. It never holds a step back, and once what it waited for is there it says "Your computer is ready", once, and goes.

1. **A welcome.** A few bots come down onto a stage, each into its own light (on the web and in the desktop app the stage is dark, whatever the app's appearance; on a phone it takes the app's), and one sentence says what they are: "A small team of bots of your own. They live on this computer, and you talk to them like people.", or, where the bots live on a server, "They work on your own machine". They are alive from the moment they land: they look at a pointer or a finger that comes close, and answer a tap. Settings stays in reach from here, for signing out or changing the language.
2. **What they do.** "They help with your day." The light goes from one bot to the next across the stage, and the bot that has it says, in its own words, one thing the bots do for the operator: "I tidy your files and folders.", "I look things up and give you the short version.", "I write and review your texts.", "I remind you, and do things on a schedule.", "I keep an eye on things for you." The last turn is everyone's, all lit: "And we work together, in a room." It goes once round, a line at a time, and rests on the last. Read by a screen reader, or under reduced motion, the six lines are one list.
3. **What they think with.** The lights go to one light the whole cast shares, and every bot thinks. "They think with an AI you already have. Claude or ChatGPT. J3 Bots installs what they need for you." So the operator knows what is being installed before anything asks them to connect it. Its button, "Meet your first bot", leads to the steps that make it.
4. **The first bot's name.** The view closes in on one of the bots, the one being made, and on the web the others step back into the dark to watch. The field starts with a person's first name, common in the language the app is in (Maya or Leo in English, Bia or Davi in Brazilian Portuguese), so the operator can just go on; they can rename the bot any time. The bot looks down at the name as it is typed, and wears it.
5. **Its look.** Twelve silhouettes and twelve colors to pick from, one pair already picked, with the bot above taking each as the operator picks, and the stage's light turning to its color. Where the bot works is not asked: J3 Bots gives every bot its own workspace.
6. **An AI, only when none is connected.** If no AI can run a bot yet, one step asks which one the bot uses, in the operator's words: "Your bots think with an AI. Use the one you already have", and answers it with the accounts themselves, one row each for **Claude** (by Anthropic) and **ChatGPT** (by OpenAI), with a quiet line under them that it uses the plan they already have, so what the bots do counts toward its limits ([decision 0085](../decisions/0085-signing-in-is-one-row-and-the-browser-opens-by-itself.md)). Each row is the account's name, and under it one line that says where it stands, with the one thing that moves it forward at the end of that line: "Not connected" beside **Sign in with Claude**. The operator signs in to the one they have, right there; the other row stays as it is. The step never says harness, CLI, engine or path; the products behind the accounts, Claude Code and Codex, appear only under Details.
   - **Signing in.** In the desktop app running the bots on its own computer, **Sign in with Claude** opens the browser by itself on Claude's sign-in page: there is no link to press. The row says "Opening the sign-in page…", then "Your browser opened. Sign in to Claude there.", with **Cancel** beside it, and under it that the screen notices on its own when they are done, with nothing to copy, and a quiet "Browser didn't open? Open the page" for the rare time it did not. Once they finish in the browser, the row says "You're signed in. Checking Claude…" and then "Connected", without a reload. From a browser or a phone, a signed-out row has **How to connect** beside "Not connected", which opens where it signs in: in the desktop app on the operator's computer, when that app runs the server, or with the one command that signs that account in, shown large with Copy, on the machine J3 Bots is installed on. An endpoint and its key stay folded away under "Advanced", in the app on the server's own computer.
   - **While it downloads.** J3 Bots downloads what each account needs itself, starting as the server starts, beside its own first download; the operator installs nothing. A download they stopped starts again from the account's button: "Sign in with Claude" in the desktop app, "Download Claude" from another device. Until it is there, the account's row says "Downloading Claude… 42%" over a thin bar that fills, and nothing else to read: the quiet line under the steps stays silent meanwhile, since the row says the same, as it does while J3 Bots is out, which the step says itself; while J3 Bots is only still starting, the rows say so and the line says how far it is. The operator does not have to wait: "Sign in with Claude" can be pressed at once, and the screen says the browser opens as soon as the download is done, with **Cancel** beside it. They can create the bot meanwhile: the app remembers what they asked for, the bot's conversation shows the same account still downloading, and the browser opens from there. Or they create the bot and sign in later.
   - **Connected** is unmistakable: the step's title says "Claude is connected", and that everything is ready for the bot to say hello, and the row shows a check.
   - **When something goes wrong**, the account's row says what in one plain sentence, with what to do next beside it, **Try again**, or **Look again** after an install the operator did themselves from the maker's page, which the row links to where J3 Bots downloads nothing for the machine. A download that failed says the true reason: the disk is full, another program such as an antivirus was using its files, or J3 Bots was not allowed to save them. A download that arrived damaged says that, not that a checksum did not match. What the engine, the server or the sign-in itself said is kept, word for word, under **Details**, with **Stop the download** there too. An error never suggests the endpoint: that stays under "Advanced" alone.
   - **It never holds the bot back needlessly.** While the accounts still ask for a press, "Create Maya and do this later" is the quiet way on under them; once an account is connected or on its way, as while it signs in, or downloads after **Sign in** was pressed, the big button creates the bot. A bot made before its AI is ready says hello once it is: the server gives it a model it lists even while that AI still downloads, and moves it to the account that connects, so an operator who signs in to ChatGPT is not left on Claude. Only a server that lists no model a bot can take refuses the bot, as while J3 Bots itself is still starting, in its first minute, so then creating waits and the step says so ("J3 Bots is still getting ready. You can create Maya in a moment."). When J3 Bots cannot run any account at all, the step says so with no choice to make: that it is restarting and the screen updates on its own; that it could not finish getting ready and keeps trying, with what the operator can do where there is something, as freeing space on a full disk; or that something on the machine it runs on needs fixing, with what under Details. The step updates by itself as the account changes, without moving on, and once shown it stays in the sequence, also when the operator goes back. It does not ask for API keys. With an AI ready, this step is skipped.
7. **The bot arrives** once the server has created it, in a short finale: its light swells and settles, the others come back into their lights and cheer, and its name comes in under the stage. Then it travels into the header of its conversation as that opens, and becomes the primary bot without being asked. On this computer, a bot asked for while J3 Bots is still starting there waits for it, and the arrival says so. A start that failed takes the screen instead: why, what repairs it, and Try again.
8. The conversation opens and **the bot speaks first**, as a new teammate would in a chat: two or three short messages, each arriving a moment after the one before while the bot is shown typing. The first says hello, with its name and who it is in a line, in its own voice; the next, two or three concrete things it could do for this operator, fitting its job; the last asks one easy question to start knowing them, such as what they would like a hand with first. If its job already says what to do, it says hello and starts the work instead, with the same approvals as any other work. It asks nothing about setup: where the operator's work is kept, which accounts, folders or connections to use, and how they like replies come up only when a task needs them. Stop works while it types, and drops what it had not posted yet. While none of the operator's bots knows what to call them, the first bot to say hello asks that, in a few friendly words, as its one question; a hello that failed asked nobody, so the next bot to say hello asks. It saves the answer as the operator's name, which every bot uses from then on, and a quiet line in the conversation says so; Settings shows the name live and still changes it. An operator who passes over the question is not asked again, and telling any bot later to call them something else changes the name the same way. On a server upgraded from before this question, the bots it already has have said hello, so no new bot asks; such an operator tells any bot what to call them, or sets it in Settings. A name the bot only reads, in a page, a file, a forwarded message or another bot's words, is never taken as theirs. It saves what matters in what the operator tells it as its first memories. There is no settings form to fill in. If the provider is not signed in yet, the hello waits for it. What the operator writes meanwhile waits too, and goes after the hello, as the bot's next turn: the bot greets, then answers the messages in the order they were written, also when the hello fails or stops at its provider's usage limit. A failed hello is otherwise offered again, until the operator writes. A bot another bot made for a room opens in that room instead, and never says hello in its own conversation: its first message in the room opens with who it is, then takes its part, paced the same way ([Several bots, one goal](#several-bots-one-goal)). If the operator writes first while nothing holds the hello back, before it has gone to the engine, the hello is dropped and the bot answers the message. Once the hello has gone, a message is like any other: it reaches the bot's turn while that turn runs and can redirect it, or goes next once it has ended.
9. The moment the operator hands it something real, the bot does the work, and asks only what that work needs.

Until its hello can run, the conversation says what it waits for: the usage limit's reset, at the time it resets when the provider gives one, or otherwise its AI being connected, with the same rows as step 6 under the bot, each with its sign-in, its download or what went wrong. The bot's row in the roster says it is getting ready. What the operator writes meanwhile waits for the hello and goes right after it, and each message says so in the same words ("Sent when the usage limit resets", "Sent once the AI is connected"); once the hello has gone, it shows as queued like any message.

### Talking to a bot

The operator picks a bot in the roster and types.

- The bot acknowledges within a second or two, in a few words, then works.
- While it works, a row where its reply will appear shows its face in that state, and one line saying, in plain words, what it is doing right now: "Running a command", "Reading a file", "Searching the web". The command itself, the file's path and the query are one step in, under each step of the work, for whoever wants them. The operator can open the work to see every step, but nothing forces them to.
- When the work has several steps, the bot's plan shows under that row without opening anything: a short card, one line a step, the done ones checked and a small live mark on the one being done. A plan of one step is not shown as one. When the turn ends the card folds into a line, "Plan done, 4 of 4", that opens the steps.
- When the bot does several things at once, it splits: it squashes, wobbles and pops out its little ones, small copies of itself, which land in a column under its face, each beside one line of what it is doing, four at most and "+2 more". A press on one, or the pointer over it, shows its line in full. One that finishes jumps back into the bot, which gulps it and swells a little. One that could not finish turns grey first, and the bot's message says what went wrong. A little one has no name and no conversation, and never speaks.
- A bot can reply while its little ones still work ("Three of me are going through the repositories; I'll put it all in one report"). They stay under its face, the row says "Working", and each goes home when it ends; once the last is home, the row folds into its line.
- The conversation's header shows the bot's face and name, with no line under the name about what it is doing: that is the conversation's to show, in the row of the turn under way and in its cards.
- The answer arrives as a short message. Longer material arrives as a file or a card.
- The operator can send another message at any time. It reaches the bot mid-turn and can redirect it.
- Typing `@` offers the operator's other bots, each by its face, and the rooms this bot is in, each by its mark, hidden ones included. A pick shows as a chip in the draft and in the message once sent, where a tap opens what it names. A mention only says whom the operator means: no one else sees the message. The bot passes on what the words ask, asking the bot named or posting in the room named, says it did, and never says one of them saw what it did not send. A message that could not go because a room it mentions was archived or the bot left it says so, and Send again sends it without that mention.
- The operator can reply to a message, the bot's or their own: a long press on a phone, or a swipe to the right, and the message's Reply button on a computer. Above the field, "Replying to Wren" shows the start of that message, with an X to drop it. To point at some words only, the operator selects them in the message and presses Quote by the selection on a computer; on a phone, Quote part in the message's menu opens its words to select from. Each quote waits above the field as a small card, up to three with the reply. Sent, the message shows them above its words, with whose they were; a tap scrolls to the original and marks it a moment, or says where it was when it is gone. The bot reads what was quoted, and in its turn may answer one older message by replying to it, rarely.
- The operator can react to any message with an emoji, the bot's or their own: a long press on a phone, the message's React button on a computer, opens a bar of six (👍 👎 ❤️ 😂 🎉 😮) and more. A reaction on the bot's message is a light signal: a few seconds later the bot sees it, and most often says nothing. It acts only when the reaction answers something it offered and it knows what that emoji means, because the operator told it ("a 👍 on an offer means go ahead"). A reaction on any other message wakes no one; the bots there see it the next time they work. A bot reacts too, now and then, the way the operator does, and its reaction notifies no one.
- Once the bot has answered, the empty composer can offer, faintly, a reply the operator might send next, with **Use** beside it ([Suggested replies](../architecture/clients.md#suggested-replies)). Use, or Tab on a computer, puts it in the message box to send or change; it never goes by itself. Typing hides it, and anything new in the conversation takes it away. Only a bot's own conversation offers one, never a room.
- "Stop" ends the work. It does not undo what was already done, and the bot says what was left running, if anything. It sits in the bot's details, one press from the conversation's header, while there is work to stop; the conversation itself stays about what the bot is doing. On a computer, `Ctrl+.` (`⌘.` on macOS) stops it from the conversation.
- If the bot's AI does not respond to Stop, Stop becomes **Force stop**, with one line saying why: the bot isn't responding to Stop, and Force stop ends what it is running now. The operator presses it once; the shortcut does the same. What the bot already did stays done, and the bot takes the next message as usual. A room offers the same while one of its bots ignores the room's Stop.

### Dictating

The operator would rather say it than type it ([decision 0096](../decisions/0096-dictation-goes-through-the-engines-speech-recognition-and-stays-on-the-operators-machines.md)).

- A microphone sits beside Send in every composer, quick access's included. A tap, or `Ctrl+Shift+Space` (`⇧⌘Space` on a Mac) on the web and in the desktop app, starts it; the words appear where the cursor was as they are heard, the ones still open to change lighter, and a small level shows it hears. Another tap stops it, and the last words settle into place. Nothing is sent: the operator reads it, fixes it, and sends it as any message. Esc, or Back on a phone, cancels, and the message box is as it was before.
- The first time on the server, it gets dictation ready once, and says how far it got in words; after that it starts at once.
- **Where the words are made** is the operator's, in Settings → Voice. Automatic: on a phone that knows the language, on the phone itself; anywhere else, on the operator's own server. The voice never leaves the operator's machines, unless they choose a cloud provider (OpenAI, xAI or OpenRouter) and give it their key, which Secrets keeps; then the words come once they stop. Settings → Voice also holds the language, the words to always spell right (names, products), taking out filler words (on), and under More the speech model, described by its size, its speed on this server and the languages it hears, and tidying up with the AI, off until turned on, which says it spends the AI's allowance.
- When it cannot, the microphone says why and what makes it work: a page opened over plain http from another machine, where browsers give no microphone (the desktop app, the Android app or an https address can); the microphone not allowed, and where to allow it; another device dictating on the server; a cloud provider without its key. The words heard before a failure stay.

### A quick word with the primary bot

On a computer with the desktop app, the operator is working in something else and wants to ask their primary bot for something.

- They press the shortcut (`Ctrl+Shift+J`, `⌘⇧J` on macOS, or the one they chose in Settings). A small card rises in the bottom-right corner of the screen the pointer is on: the conversation in miniature, with the bot's face beside its name and what it is doing, the end of its conversation and the message box, with the cursor already in it.
- They type and press Enter. The card stays where it is, above their other windows, while the bot answers; they can go back to their work and look at it when they like.
- It is the bot's own conversation, not a separate one: what they read here is read, what they typed and did not send is still in the message box in the main window, and a question or an approval the bot shows can be answered here.
- A file dropped on the card, or an image pasted, is attached to the message.
- Above the message box, a chip names what they were in when they pressed the shortcut: the app and its window's title, such as "Example Browser · Invoice 2026-031". A tap shows it whole, with a line saying the bot gets it with the message; its × takes it off, and then nothing of that window goes. It goes with the next message only, and is gone once that is sent.
- Beside it, a dashed "Picture" chip. A tap takes a picture of that window and shows it in the chip; a tap on the chip shows it larger, and its × takes it off. It goes as an image attached to the message. Settings can have it taken every time; it starts off. On a Mac, the first time, a card says that macOS lets J3 Bots see other apps' windows only with Screen Recording, and offers to open System Settings; until it is allowed, the chip names the app alone. If the picture cannot be taken, the chip says so, and a tap tries again.
- In Settings, This device has a switch for the window's title (on) and one for a picture with every message (off). On Wayland, Settings says the desktop does not let an app see another's window, and no chip shows. Opened from the tray's menu or over J3 Bots itself, no chip shows either.
- In the conversation, on any device, a message that went with a window says so under it: "Looking at Example Browser · Invoice 2026-031". Rooms show it the same.
- `Escape`, the card's close button or the shortcut again put it away. Clicking elsewhere does not: they put it there. Pressing the shortcut while it is on screen but they are working elsewhere brings the cursor back to it.
- They can drag it by its top to another place; it opens there next time on that screen.
- "Open conversation" brings the main window to that conversation, for what needs the room: its details, its files, its routines. What a card in it leads to opens there too, where it would from the conversation: "Open browser" on the tab the bot asks help with, a connector's sign-in, a routine's runs, a room, what two bots wrote each other.
- Before there is a primary bot, or while J3 Bots cannot be reached, the shortcut opens the main window instead, which says what is missing.

Quick access shows only the primary bot, and only while it is open: nothing of J3 Bots sits on the screen otherwise. On an Android phone, sharing and the home-screen widget take its place (below).

### From other apps and the home screen

On an Android phone, the operator is reading something in another app and wants a bot to have it, or wants to see at a glance how a bot or a routine is doing.

- In any app's share menu (a photo, a page, a PDF, some text, one thing or several), J3 Bots is listed, and so are the bots they share to most, each with its face. Either opens a small sheet over the app they are in; the app they were in stays where it was.
- The sheet shows the start of what they are sharing, or how many files, then their bots as faces with names: the one they shared to last first, then the pinned ones, then the rest. A bot picked from the share menu is already picked; if it is no longer one they can send to, nothing is picked and Send asks them to pick a bot. Below, "Add a message", which they can leave empty, and "Send to Ada".
- Send sends it as one message, as if they had attached it in the conversation and pressed Send there: their line first, then what was shared. A shared page arrives as its title and its address; J3 Bots never opens the page. Whatever they were writing to that bot in the app stays as it was. The sheet goes and a short note says "Sent to Ada".
- Files keep the conversation's limits. A file too large or of a kind a bot cannot take is named with why, and the rest go; a file that could not be read is counted, "1 file couldn't be read, so it stays behind".
- While files go, the sheet says how far along it is, and Stop ends it. If the bots cannot be reached, it says so and keeps going until they can be. Once the files are up, the message goes as any message they send, and Stop is gone. A share of more files than one message takes says how many stay behind.
- If the phone is not connected to their bots yet, the sheet says so and offers "Open J3 Bots". With no bots yet, it says there is none to send to.
- A bot template file shared on its own opens J3 Bots on the template, to make a bot from it.
- They can put a J3 Bots widget on the home screen. As they add it, it asks what it should show: a bot, or a bot's routine. A bot widget shows its face and, when there is room, "Working", "Waiting for you" or "Quiet"; a tap opens its conversation. A routine widget shows its name, "Ran 2 hours ago" and, when there is room, the start of what it said last; a tap opens that, in the conversation or in the routine's runs.
- The widget can be made as small as one cell or as large as they like, and follows the phone's light or dark look. Holding it and choosing to change it opens the same question.
- The widget shows what the app last saw: it updates while the app is open and connected, and on Android's own refresh. A bot that was working shows quiet once that is half an hour old, since it may have finished unseen.
- A bot or routine that was removed shows "This bot was removed" with "Pick another". Before the phone is connected, the widget says so and opens the app.

Neither exists on iPhone.

### Showing a bot your screen

On the computer, showing a bot how something is done can be quicker than telling it.

- In the desktop app, `+` in the composer offers **Record your screen** beside files; so does quick access, which opens the app's window for it. A bot can offer it too, under its message, when the operator describes something they do on the computer. Elsewhere it isn't offered, and a bot's offer says to open the app on the computer.
- A short sheet says what happens, in plain words: all their screens, their voice, and the keys they press and where they click are recorded until they stop; it goes into their message to the bot as a file, and the bot's AI looks at it; nothing leaves the computer while they record; in their message it uploads to their J3 Bots server as any file does, and the bot gets it when they send. It says how passwords are kept: where the system says one is being typed, only that one was typed; elsewhere, to pause before typing one. On Wayland it says only the screen they share when the system asks and their voice are recorded. Below, the microphone, with bars that move as they speak, and **No microphone**. On a Mac, the first time, it lists the two permissions macOS asks for, Screen Recording and Accessibility, each with a button that opens System Settings at it. **Start recording** counts 3, 2, 1.
- The app's windows step aside, and a small bar floats over everything: the bot's face, listening, a red dot, the voice's bars, and three buttons, Discard, Pause and Stop. There is no clock. It can be dragged out of the way. Pause records nothing until Resume, which is how to hide something. Discard asks once more, in the bar itself. When the disk runs low, the bar shows it, and the recording stops by itself before the disk fills, keeping what it has.
- On Stop the window comes back with the recording in the draft, as a file named for when it started. First its voice is written down, the way dictation hears it: the file shows a voice in place of its icon, and its bar fills as that goes ("Writing down what you said, 40%" when pointed at); then it uploads. Where dictation can't run on this computer, or the server has no speech model yet, it uploads at once, without a transcript. The operator can write around it and send it as any message, or remove it. A recording that stopped because the app quit is kept, and offered in the draft the next time the app opens, with a line that says so.
- The bot receives a `.zip` it can read without watching: a video of each screen, the voice and what was said in it, with times, what was clicked and typed and in which window, a picture at each click, and a README that says how to read it all.

### Being asked

A bot needs a decision or permission.

- A **question** arrives as a card with a few options and, when it makes sense, room for a typed answer. Tapping an option answers it. A question from the bot's coding tool shows whole, however many parts it has, one under the other, each with the few words its tool puts over it and, under an option, the sketch or snippet the tool shows of it.
- An **approval** arrives as a card that names the exact action, with Allow and Deny. Allowing can be for this once or for the session.
- A **credential** arrives as a card that names what is asked for and why, with an input for each value it needs, all at once: "Sign in to Mail" asks for the email and the password together. A password, key or code is hidden as it is typed, with an eye that shows it; something that is not secret, like an email, shows as typed. A sign-in's email and password can be filled by the operator's password manager. The card says where the values go: kept from the bot, which uses them without seeing them, or in private files the bot can read for the next 24 hours. Enter moves to the next empty value and sends once all are filled; Send waits until every one is. Decline tells the bot it carries on without them. Once answered, here or on another device, or once the bot stops waiting, the card only says what happened: nothing typed shows in the conversation, a notification or the bot's words. It works the same in quick access.
- All three produce a notification on the operator's devices. Answering on any device resolves the card everywhere.
- While the bot waits on a card the operator has scrolled away from, "Waiting for you" floats over the end of the conversation, with an arrow toward the card, and brings it back into view.
- A question the bot asked on its own can be dismissed, which is a "no". The bot does not ask again. A question its coding tool asks mid-task has no dismiss: it waits for an answer, or for the operator to stop the bot.

### Watching the bot's browser, and taking it over

A bot shops, books or reads a site in its own browser, on the machine J3 Bots runs on.

- While the bot has a page open, a globe sits in its conversation's header, its mark breathing while the bot acts on the page. Under the line of the turn under way, a picture of the page and the site's name, taken again as the page changes and every few seconds while the bot works. It is a picture, not a live view: watching it costs nothing.
- Either opens the browser: beside the conversation on a wide window, over it on a tablet, rising from the bottom on a phone. The page is live, its site and address shown, and a line says who is acting on it: "The bot is browsing", "You have control" or "On another device". While the operator only watches, the page takes nothing they do, and the bot's pointer shows where it acts.
- **Take control** is the one primary action. Once taken, the bot cannot act on the page, and everything the operator does over it goes to the page: the pointer, the wheel, typing, a phone's keyboard on a field they tap. Back, forward, reload and the address field appear only now. **Done** gives the page back to the bot.
- When the bot asked for help on the page (a sign-in, a code, a check that a person is there), **Done** tells it the step is done, and **Can't do it** tells it the operator could not; either gives the page back. **Open browser** on that card opens the browser on the page. Its notification says what the step is and the site, "Needs your help in the browser: Sign in on example.com", never the bot's own note, so a link or a code the bot put there stays off a locked screen.
- A sign-in window the page opens shows in the browser as the bot's front page; the operator keeps control in it, and the browser goes back to the page it came from when it closes.
- Leaving the browser while in control asks once whether to give the page back to the bot. Keeping it leaves the bot waiting until the operator returns and presses Done; giving it back this way leaves a help card open, since the step may not be done.
- The operator's clipboard and files never reach the page: pasting, dropping, downloads and file pickers are out of reach in this version.

### Work that starts on its own

The operator says "every weekday morning, tell me what changed in the repo" or "let me know when the deploy finishes".

- The bot creates a routine and confirms the exact schedule it saved, in the operator's time zone.
- When the routine runs and has something to report, a message appears in the bot's conversation. It reads like the bot mentioning something it took care of, not like a system alert.
- When there is nothing to report, nothing appears. The run is still recorded in the routine's history.
- The operator pauses, edits, tests or deletes a routine from the bot's details, or by telling the bot.

The operator says "tell me when Ana sends an invoice" to a bot that uses their mail through a connector.

- The bot looks at what the connector can tell (its events) and, when one fits, sets a routine that runs as it happens, not on a schedule. The operator copies no address, picks no setting and touches no connector: J3 Bots listens for the event and chooses how.
- The routine reads in the operator's words: "When Mail says a new email from Ana arrives". It pauses, opens its runs and is deleted like any routine.
- Several events close together make one run with all of them; nothing arrives for an event the instruction says to pass over.
- When J3 Bots can't reach the connector for a while, the routine says it is waiting for it. When it has to stop listening, because access was taken back or the connector no longer offers the event, the routine says so and why, and the bot tells the operator in its next message and offers to set it again.

The operator says "every Monday, gather my emails, have Atlas draft the replies, and ask me before Wren sends them".

- The bot builds a routine in steps, naming its teammates, and its change card shows the Track at rest: one sentence that reads the routine back ("Every Monday at 9:00, Scout gathers last week's emails, only if there's something new Atlas summarises them and drafts replies, then, with your yes, Wren sends the replies"), composed from the steps, never by a model, and the stations under it. A one-step routine looks as it always did.
- When it runs, one card in the owner's conversation follows it: the light moves down the rail, each bot step shows its bot at work and then its one result line ("14 new emails"), and each bot shows as working in its own roster row, its work in its own conversation.
- A condition that does not hold ends the run quietly, with what the bot found ("Nothing new"); the steps after it show as skipped.
- At the operator's yes the card turns to the waiting color and asks the question the bot before wrote ("Send the 3 replies Atlas drafted?"), with its two answers, a note and how long it waits. The owner shows as waiting in the roster, and a notification leads to the card; nothing is answered from the notification. No, or no answer in time, ends the run quietly.
- A step that failed reads in plain words with one main action: Try again, which resumes from that step and says first when the step may have done part of its work, or what the bot said blocked it. Stop, while the run goes, ends it there.
- Once a run ends, its card folds to one line ("Done · 3 replies sent"), which opens the Track again. The routine's runs show each run as a small rail of dots, and the details show the bots that take part and the sentence.

### Several bots, one goal

The operator asks a bot for a team, or sets one up themselves.

- **Asking for one.** The operator tells a bot, in its conversation, something like "make me a support group, several bots". In that one turn, the bot makes the teammates the group lacks (only the primary bot can, at most five at a time), each named as a colleague would be, unless the operator named them: a first name common where the operator lives and in their language, different from the other bots' names, with the role in its title, never in the name. Each runs on the bot's own model, or on the default one while that model's provider is not ready. The bot then starts a room with them, and posts a kickoff there: what the room is for, who owns which part, the rules, and what each member should answer first. Then it says in the conversation what it set up and who owns what, under a quiet "Scout started Support" line. A bot made for the room opens there, with no hello in its own conversation: its first message in the room says who it is, and its conversation shows instead a quiet "Made by Scout for Support" line. Both lines name the room as it is called now, so a rename shows there, and open it with a tap; once it is deleted they say "a room" and open nothing. The members the kickoff names, every one by default, start on it at once; a member the kickoff gives a part answers it rather than waiting for someone else to speak. Only the operator's own word starts a team: a bot never makes teammates or rooms because a routine, another bot or something it read asked.
- **Setting one up.** The operator creates a room, adds two to six bots, and writes.

Either way, the room then works the same:

- The room's header shows its members' avatars side by side, up to three, then how many more ("+2"), and its name. The composer reads "Message Support".
- Every member reads every message in the room, the operator's and each bot's. A message that mentions nobody wakes every member at once, but the bot that wrote it, and each answers only if it has something to add, so a hello is not answered by every member. Members answering at the same moment do not see each other's replies, so two may still say the same thing, as people in a group do. The room's empty state says so.
- `@Name` speaks to that bot: only the bots a message names are woken, and they answer; the others read it the next time they are woken, with whatever came after it. `@everyone` wakes every member and asks each to answer. A bot's message can name members too, and wakes only them; one that names nobody wakes the others. A reply to a bot's message that names nobody speaks to that bot, as if it named it; a quoted passage speaks to no one in particular. Nothing under the composer says whom a message goes to: a picked mention stands out in the text as a chip with the bot's face and color, as it does in the message once sent. A tap on a mention in a message opens what it names: a bot's conversation, or a room. The operator's message reaches a member it wakes mid-turn when that member is already answering in the room, and can redirect it; what a member missed while it worked reaches it with its next turn.
- While members answer, one quiet line at the room's end says who, with their faces: "Hal is answering…", several at once, "Hal and Bo are answering…", and past three, "Hal, Bo, and 2 more are answering…". It changes in place as each one's turn ends and goes once nobody answers, whether or not they said anything.
- A member whose turn there has a plan, or has split into little ones, shows them at the room's end, under its face and name, as its own conversation would. They show only in the room, never in the member's own conversation, and go when its turn ends, or once the last little one it handed off is home.
- A message that could not go because a member it mentions left the room says so, and Send again sends it to the members it mentions who stayed, or to the room when none did.
- Bots answer each other in the room. A member also posts there from its own conversation: when the operator asks it there to start work in the room or tell the room something, it posts it itself and says it did, instead of asking the operator to write in the room. What the members said there is part of its conversation, so it answers about the room from memory, and reads the room's latest messages, or searches them, when it does not remember or needs an exact detail. It never posts from its conversation on a stale view: if others wrote in the room since it last saw it, it is shown what it missed first, and when that changes what the operator asked (the work is already done, someone already answered), it tells the operator instead of posting. It quiets down when no bot has anything to add. The room also counts rounds of bots answering each other since the operator last wrote there, the same in a room of two as in a room of six: the members' replies to the operator are the first round, the replies to those the second, and so on. From the third round its members are told the conversation is winding down and answer only what is essential; past the fourth, a bot's message is still posted but wakes nobody, and one quiet line says the bots stopped answering each other and the room waits for the operator. The operator's next message there starts the rounds again, and so does the first post they asked a member for in its own conversation, so a room that went quiet starts again from there too. A request sent while the member was already busy with something else does not count yet: the member posts it, and asks the operator to ask again once it has finished if the room stayed quiet.
- Approvals and questions never appear in a room. They go to the asking bot's own conversation, and the room shows that the bot is waiting.
- While the room's work is under way, also work a bot left running in the background for it, the room's details offer Stop. It asks first, saying what ends (what the bots are doing for the room, and the work they left running for it) and that anything else they're doing goes on.

### One bot hands work to another

- The operator asks, or the bot offers ("want me to ask Scout?").
- What the two bots say to each other goes in their **teammate conversation**, "Scout and Wren", one per pair of bots. It reads like a room: each bot's name over its messages, its avatar in its tint, the time between them, and each message that never reached the other bot marked so. The operator reads it and cannot write in it: in place of a composer, a "Read-only" mark. Its menu offers "Talk to Scout", "Talk to Wren" and "Start a room with both".
- In each bot's own conversation, a run of messages with teammates, with nothing else between them, however long it lasted, is one small line led by the first teammate's small avatar ("Wrote to Wren", "Wren wrote", "Talked with Wren · 3 messages"), which opens the teammate conversation at the run. A run with several teammates ("Talked with Wren and Pip · 4 messages") first asks which conversation to open, in a sheet on the phone. A line whose messages did not all reach their teammate says how many did not, quietly ("1 not delivered"). An urgent message that never reached the teammate still shows the recipient's conversation a card that offers to send it again.
- The reply comes back later, as its own turn. Messages between bots never make a conversation unread, never become its last line in the roster and never notify: the operator is told only when a bot has something new for them, and a bot that needs them says so.

### Changing a bot

- **Name, title, avatar, model, permission mode**: in the bot's details.
- **Effort**, right under the model: "Default", then the levels that model offers, such as "Low", "Medium", "High", with one line on the trade: more effort takes longer and uses more of the plan, for more careful work, and Default lets the AI decide. Default is where every bot starts, and it sends nothing, so the AI works as it would on its own. A model with no levels to pick shows no choice at all. When the operator picks a model that does not offer the bot's level, the choice goes back to Default and a line under it says so in plain words ("GPT-5.5 Codex doesn't offer Max, so Scout uses Default"); the same line shows when a model stops offering a level the bot had. A turn never fails over it.
- **Job**: an editable text in the bot's details. Telling the bot "from now on, never do X" updates memory instead; the job stays a short statement of purpose.
- **Memory**: the operator can read what a bot remembers, correct a line or delete it. They can also just tell the bot to forget something.
- **Fresh start**: stops any work the bot has running and resets its working context, with nothing of the recent conversation carried over, and drops the notes that would have told it what its routines reported. The conversation history, memory, routines and files stay. Each routine keeps what its recent runs said, which is the routine's own state: a run reads it to tell what is new and not report the same thing again. Moving a bot to another provider stops its work the same way, and its new working context does carry a digest of the recent conversation.
- **Delete**: removes the bot, its conversation, memory and routines after an explicit confirmation. Files in its workspace are kept unless the operator chooses otherwise.

### Sharing a bot, and starting from one

The operator has a bot that does its job well and wants someone else to have it, or a copy to start from.

- **Share as template…**, in the bot's details and in its roster menu under More, shows everything the file will hold, in full: the profile, the job, each skill's text, each routine's instruction and schedule, the model and the permission mode. It says that the job, the skills and the routines go exactly as written, and that the bot may have written some of them, so the operator reads them for anything they would not share; one line says what never goes (memory, the conversation, files, accounts, credentials, anything else on this machine). A bot whose file would be too large to read is not shared: the screen says so, and names the largest part to shorten. On Android it ends in the system's share sheet with a `.j3bot` file; on the web and the desktop the file is downloaded, or its text copied.
- **New bot from a template** in the roster's new menu picks a `.j3bot` file. A file dropped anywhere on the web or desktop window, shared to J3 Bots on Android, or tapped where it kept its media type, leads to the same place.
- That place is a preview of everything the bot will be and do, with a note that the file came from outside J3 Bots, that what it says goes only into the new bot, and that the other bots see the new one as any teammate. It says which model the bot will run on here, and whether that replaces the template's because this server does not offer it. The permission mode starts at the template's or the default, whichever lets the bot do less, and says so when it lowered the template's; the operator can pick any mode. A mode left as it was is the server's to settle when the bot is made, by the same rule. Its routines arrive paused, to be turned on in its details.
- Nothing is created until the operator presses Create. Then the new bot's conversation opens, and it says hello as any new bot does, the things it offers coming from its job, without starting any of that work, since the template's author wrote the job, not the operator. It begins when the operator asks. A file that is too large, damaged, or from a newer J3 Bots is refused with a sentence saying which.

What a template holds and leaves out is in [Concepts](concepts.md) and [decision 0023](../decisions/0023-a-bot-template-is-a-file.md).

### Giving a bot a service

The operator wants a bot to reach something it cannot yet: their notes, their tracker, their calendar.

- **Connectors**, in Settings as **MCP and skills** and in a bot's details, opens one screen that lists what the bots added: the operator's connectors first, each with its status and who may use it, and, once its server answered, a line with the version of MCP it speaks and how many tools, resources and prompts it offers. Each says who changed it last and when: the operator, or which bot. The skills follow, those every bot shares, then each bot's own, each with the same line. J3 Bots' own skills (`j3-…`) are not listed: every bot has them, and nobody changes them. With no connector, or no skill, one line says to ask a bot to add one. There is no form to add either, and no gallery to browse: the operator asks a bot.
- **The operator asks a bot**: "connect my Linear". The bot searches the gallery and adds it; in Supervised the operator approves the step first. A key is never typed in the conversation: the bot asks for it as a secret and uses only its name. A connector that needs signing in shows a Sign in card in the conversation, and the bot says it waits for that. A service that signs in no person, only a client the operator registers there (a build server, say), shows no Sign in card: the bot asks for that client's values on one card, "Client ID" shown as typed and "Client secret" hidden, with a link to the page where the service issues them, and once they are saved it connects with them, never seeing them. A server in the operator's own network, or one that needs an app the operator registers (Google, Slack), waits on their yes: a card in the conversation says it in plain words ("Ana wants to connect to a service on your own network at 192.168.1.20:8123. Allow?"), or shows how to set up the app and takes its client ID and secret, which the bot never sees; Allow and Decline. Once answered, or closed with no answer, the card says how it ended, and the bot is told and carries on. The primary bot adds one for every bot; any other bot adds one for itself. Once a connector uses a secret, no bot changes where it sends it (its address, headers, command, arguments or other variables): the bot says the operator can change a command on its page, or offers to add it again and ask for the secret anew.
- **A skill** is one a bot saved or installed, when the operator asked or agreed. A bot asked to remember a procedure offers to save it as a skill, and does when the operator agrees. A skill's page shows its text to change, where it came from at which commit, and who changed it last, and Remove asks first.
- On the phone, a connector that runs a command shows read-only, with a line that it is changed or removed from J3 Bots on the computer: the phone is no place to review a command that runs on the server. Its status, and why it is not running, read as on the computer.
- A command connector's page, on the web and the desktop, has **Change command**: its program and its variables' names as they are, its arguments and values never shown. A new value alone is saved as it is, an empty one keeping its own. Anything else (the program, the arguments, a variable's name, one added or removed, or a new value for a variable that decides what code runs) needs the arguments and each value typed again, since the command line is saved whole, and asks for a confirmation first: the whole command line, every argument as it will run, a variable that decides what code runs (such as `NODE_OPTIONS` or `PYTHONPATH`) with its value, above the program and marked as part of the line, and the name of every other variable, never its value, marking a variable that is new, one that is gone and a program that changed, and saying it runs there as the operator's user, with everything that user can reach. Before that, the form says what the server would refuse: a package `npx` or `uvx` fetches at no exact version (with an example of one), a launcher option J3 Bots cannot read past, a variable such as `PATH` that chooses which program runs, or one named twice. Nothing runs until they choose Run it. The process the old command started stops, and the new one lists its tools, which bots get as for any connector. Disconnect says the program stops and its variables' values are deleted.
- A connector's page shows its status and what fixes it: connected; needs signing in again (Sign in again); can't reach it, and why; a command that is not running, because its program is not on the computer (install it, or name it by its full path), because it stopped or could not start (check the command and its variables; it starts again when a bot next uses it, waiting longer each time), or because J3 Bots refuses to start it as it is saved, as when a variable that decides which code runs takes its value from a secret (change the command and confirm it again); needs a secret, naming the one its settings use that is missing, has no value or is not shared with every bot that uses the connector (Open Secrets, to save or share it; choosing fewer bots works too), or whose credentials the service refused (a bot asks for new ones); its sign-in now goes somewhere other than where its credentials were given for, where J3 Bots doesn't send them (remove it and ask a bot to add it again); or secrets unavailable, when the server cannot read its secrets, which keep every connector's address or command, so every connector shows it then, one that needs no key included (Open Secrets, which opens Settings over the screen; closing Settings returns to the page as it was, with anything typed in it). Its tools are listed once it is connected. It says which bots use it, changed in place, and the origin of its address or the program it runs, never the whole address or the arguments, which can carry a key. Disconnect asks first.
- A connector's tools are what its service offers now: one it adds, changes or removes reaches bots as it does, with nothing to accept. Its page lists them.
- **A service's skills**: some services also offer skills, instructions for a kind of task, such as drafting an invoice their way. There is nothing to set: every bot that may use the connector sees them, and loads one when a task fits it, asking first as its permission mode says. The connector's page lists them under **Skills it offers bots**, by name and what each is for. While a bot loads one, its work line says so: "Using the skill invoices from Billing". A skill the service changed, or that is not what it listed, is not used, and the bot says so.
- **A service can ask the operator** in the middle of what a bot asked of it: a card in that bot's conversation, which notifies, says which service asks and that the answer goes to the service only, never to the bot. It is a short form, or a page of the service's to open, named by its address's host; a service that needs a step finished on its own page before it can answer asks the same way, and the bot's request goes again once the service says the step is done. Send or Open, Decline, or Not now; the bot waits meanwhile. A form never asks for a password, a key or a code: J3 Bots declines one that does before it is shown. The card says when it is no longer needed, because the bot's work ended.
- **A service's prompts** are in the composer of a bot's conversation: typing `/` lists those of the connectors that bot may use, by name, with the service beside each. Picking one asks for its arguments, if it has any, and puts the text the service writes in the draft, to read, change and send as the operator's own.
- **A service's app.** Some services draw an interface for what a bot asked of them, such as a stock list or a map. It is part of the bot's reply, where the bot used the service, under a small header with the service's mark and name and what the bot used. It is as tall as it needs to be, within limits, and the operator uses it in place ([decision 0093](../decisions/0093-a-connectors-app-is-part-of-the-bots-reply.md)).
  - **Full screen.** The header's expand control, or the app itself, shows it over the conversation. The composer stays under it, and what the bot says meanwhile slides up over the app's lower part, to read or hide. The close control, Esc or the phone's Back puts it back in the reply.
  - **Pinned.** The app can ask to stay at the top of the conversation while the operator scrolls. Its place in the reply says where it went, with **Put it back here**.
  - **What it asks.** It never acts as the operator without them: running a tool as the bot outside full access (**Allow**, **Don't allow**), sending the bot a message, opening a link and saving files each wait on a button of the app's own line under it. Saving gives the browser's download, or the phone's share sheet.
  - **What the bot learns.** What the operator does in it can reach the bot the next time it answers there, as the service's words, never as the operator's. It wakes no one and shows nowhere as a message.
  - **Out of view.** An app far up the conversation stops running, and comes back at its size when the operator scrolls to it.
- A bot that finds its connector signed out says so with a card in its conversation, whose Sign in again opens the connector's page and starts the sign-in, or opens its form again for one that takes a key. That form starts empty, since no secret is shown again, and only what the operator types in it changes: a new key alone keeps the account or workspace typed beside it before.

What a connector is, and what a bot can and cannot do with one, is in [Concepts](concepts.md); how its secrets are kept is in [Security: connectors](../architecture/security.md#connectors).

### Knowing what it costs

- A usage view shows each provider's limit windows as the engine reports them, and which bots and routines used turns recently.
- A routine that fires often shows its run count next to its schedule.
- When a provider's limit is reached, affected bots show as blocked with the reset time, and their routines wait instead of failing repeatedly.
- The suggested reply after each answer costs one more small request to the AI account for each turn a bot finishes, and Settings → Providers can turn it off, saying so in those words.
- Dictation tidied up by the AI costs one small request for each dictation; it is off until the operator turns it on in Settings → Voice, which says so. Dictation itself costs nothing on the operator's own server, and a cloud provider bills the operator's own key.

### Keeping J3 Bots up to date

- A newer release can't be missed. On a paired device, one dialog opens in front of the app: "J3 Bots {version} is available", one sentence on what updating does, a few lines on what's new, a link to the release notes, "Update now" and "Later". It opens the same way on the web, the desktop and the phone, for the server's release and for the app's own.
- "Update now" updates the server first, then this app. It asks first only when bots have work running, as that work stops, with "Stop and update". The dialog shows both steps: the server's as it goes (downloading, checking the download against the project's signature, getting it ready, restarting), then the app's once the server answers on the new version. The desktop app restarts into its new version, the Android app installs its own, and a browser reloads. With the server already current, only the app updates. While the server restarts, the connection line says "Updating your bots to {version}…", not that the connection was lost, and the app reconnects by itself.
- When the server's update doesn't go through, the app is left as it was. The dialog says why, gives the command to run when there is one, and offers "Try again". An app that doesn't update after its server did offers "Try again" for itself.
- "Later" puts the dialog away for a day, and a newer release brings it back sooner. One quiet line stays at the foot of the roster, above Settings (over the roster on the phone), and opens it again. A step that runs can be hidden too, and the dialog comes back when it needs the operator. Settings → About and updates keeps the versions and the channels, and its "Update now" opens the same dialog.
- How it ended is said once in a toast, and shows in Settings until the operator closes it after: "Your bots run {version}", or that the update didn't go through and the bots are back on the version before, with their data as it was. A rollback stays in Settings, with why, its details and "Try again", for as long as the server runs the version it rolled back to.
- A server that cannot update itself from a client (started by hand, run from a source checkout, run inside the desktop app elsewhere) says so, with the one command to run on its machine instead. No button is ever offered that cannot work.
- Each side has a channel, Stable or Nightly, chosen in the same place: the server's for the server, the app's for the desktop app. Moving back to Stable never installs an older version: a nightly build stays until a stable release is newer, and Settings says so.
- The desktop app updates itself, with nothing to press. A newer version found as it opens is installed before anything else shows: the window says "Updating to version {version}", with the download's progress, and the app opens again by itself on the new version. With no network, or a release that takes more than a few seconds to answer, it opens as usual and says nothing. One found while it runs is offered in the dialog, whose "Update now" restarts into it after the server's update; put off with "Later", it installs as the operator quits, and "Restart to update" in Settings brings it sooner. A restart waits for changes being saved, and says so with "Cancel restart" beside it, for an upload stuck offline. A check that could not reach the release, as on a laptop opened before its Wi-Fi connects, tries again within minutes and when the computer wakes. An install that fails says so and offers the new version's download. "Update automatically" in Settings, on at first, turns off the install at launch and at quit: then the update waits for "Update now" or "Restart to update". Nothing installs that has not matched the project's signature. On this computer the app carries the bots' server, so updating the app updates both, and the bots start once the update at launch is done.
- After an update, **What's new** says what you can now do, in a few plain lines per feature, the newest release first. It is always one tap away: at the foot of the roster, above Settings, on the web and the desktop, and at the end of Settings on the phone. Once the server runs a release with something new, it is marked "New in 0.14" until opened, and on the phone a quiet line over the roster says so too. Nothing opens by itself. An app newer than its server only tells what that server can do.
- When the app and the server speak different versions, the screen says which one is behind. A server behind the app offers "Update the server" right there, after asking it to look for a newer release once, as it may not have looked since this app's release came out; a server that cannot update from here gives the command to run on its machine; an app behind the server updates itself, or a browser reloads. The operator is never left on a broken screen.

### Moving the bots to a server

- Bots that started on the operator's computer move to a server that stays on from Settings → Where your bots live → "Move to another server". One dialog says what goes (the conversations, memory, skills and files, the shared memory and skills, attachments, routines, rooms and secrets) and what stays: paired devices and provider sign-ins, so the operator pairs and signs in again there, and each webhook gets a new address. It asks for a passphrase for the secrets, twice, and says plainly that it can't be recovered. Then the system's save dialog asks where the file goes.
- While it exports, the dialog says so. An export that fails says why, in the export's own words, and the bots run on the computer again as before; the operator can try again at once.
- Once the file is written, the bots stop on the computer and the app says they moved: when, the file, anything the export left out (secrets it could not read, with what brings them, files whose name a server refuses, by name), and what to do on the new server: import the file, pair again, give each webhook its new address. They stay stopped, even when the computer restarts, so they never run in two places by surprise.
- From there, three ways on. "Connect to the new server" goes to its address and pairing. "Run the bots here again" asks first, then starts them on the computer as they were. "Delete the data on this computer" asks first, saying the file is their only copy until the new server runs them, and that the secrets are lost with it when the file left them out. Connected to the new server, Settings still says the old bots' data is on this computer, with the same way to delete it.

## Screens

The interface is the same three regions on every surface. On a phone they are three screens; on a wide window they sit side by side.

### Roster

Answers: who do I have, who needs me, what is each of them doing.

- One row per bot and per room: avatar, name, time of last activity, one line of status or the last message.
- Rows that need the operator (a pending question or approval, an unread result) come first and are marked.
- Pinned bots stay at the top, as large avatars with their names; a room keeps its own pin, at the top of the list.
- The operator can group bots in named sections, in an order they choose, each one folded or open. A bot is in one section at most, and deleting a section never deletes its bots: they return to the list.
- Hidden bots and rooms keep running, and still notify. They are left out of the list and reachable through search, through everything that names them (a notification, a mention, a notice, a teammate list), and through a "Hidden conversations" row at its end, which says how many there are and whether one has something unread, and lists them, each with Show. A hidden one's details say it is hidden, with Show.
- Whether a bot or room is hidden is set when it is made and when it is edited: "Hide from the list", off unless the operator turns it on, in New bot, New bot from a template, Edit, and the room's form. A bot that builds a team may hide the helpers it makes that the operator did not ask to see, and say so; once the operator has shown or hidden something, or made it themselves, no bot changes that.
- After the bots and rooms, a "Between bots" section lists each pair of bots that wrote each other, latest first: both avatars overlapped, "Scout and Wren", the last message with who said it ("Wren: …") and its time. It is not one of the operator's sections, and its rows are never unread, pinned or notified. A pair shows only while both bots are in the list, neither hidden nor deleted; the other bot's details still reach it.
- Each bot and room has a menu (right-click, the "…" or a long press) to mark it read or unread, pin it, move it to a section, make it the primary bot, hide or show it, and reach its details. The primary bot's avatar carries a small star wherever it appears.
- Search finds bots, rooms and messages, and pairs of bots by either name, hidden ones too.
- New bot and new room are one action each, and a new bot can start from a template file.
- A bot or a room that was just made, by the operator or by a bot, comes down into its row in a light of its own color and lands there, a room with its members' faces, as the rows under it make room; its name and line come in as it lands, and the light goes down. Under reduced motion it fades in. One that is deleted or hidden sinks away as the others close up. Nothing of this plays for the rows the roster opens with.
- A bot that has split carries a tiny dot by its avatar for each little one at work, four at most, also after it replied while they still work. What they are doing is in its conversation.
- The bots in the rows are alive: each blinks, breathes and now and then does something small of its own, looks at a pointer or a finger that comes close to it and at nothing further away, goes down under a press and answers a tap.

### Conversation

Answers: what did we say, what is it doing, what does it need from me.

- Messages as bubbles, the operator's on one side and the bot's on the other, with timestamps between groups.
- What a message replies to or quotes sits above its words, as small cards with the face and name of whoever said it and the start of the message or the words quoted, at most two lines each. A tap goes to the original.
- When the operator describes a task they do on their computer, a bot can now and then offer to watch them do it: under its message, one quiet button, **Record your screen**, that opens the recorder in the desktop app and its quick access. In a browser, and on a phone, which cannot record, a quiet line takes its place: "To record your screen, open the J3 Bots app on your computer." The recording goes to the bot as a file, like any other.
- Reactions sit under a message as small pills, one per emoji, with a count when more than one person or bot chose it. The operator's own looks chosen, and a tap on it takes it off; picking the same emoji again does the same, and picking another replaces it, one reaction each per message. Who reacted is a press on the pill away on a phone, the pointer over it on a computer. A bot that looked at a reaction and said nothing leaves nothing behind in the conversation, and a room names it among those answering only once it speaks.
- In a room, each bot's name stands over its run of messages and its avatar beside the last, as in a teammate conversation. The header shows the members' avatars, three at most and "+2" for the rest, the room's name and, under it, who is in it. While members answer, the room's last line names them ("Hal is answering…").
- In a bot's conversation, the header shows the bot's face and name on one line, with no line about what it is doing under it.
- Cards inline for what needs the operator (questions, approvals, credentials, a step in the bot's browser, a connector to add or sign in to) and for files. A picture a message carries shows as a photo, as one a person sends; a tap opens it large. A document a message carries (a PDF, a text or Markdown file, a table, a spreadsheet, a Word document or a presentation) shows the top of its first page or its first rows in its card, with what it is in words ("PDF, 12 pages"); a tap opens it in a viewer inside the app, with the same open and close as a picture's. A document too big to show, or one the app cannot read, says so and offers to save it, or on a phone to open it in another app.
- What only happened takes one quiet line, as small as a time between messages: messages with teammates ("Talked with Wren · 3 messages"), a routine created or changed ("Routine Morning digest created"; several in a row, "3 routines created"), a routine that stopped listening for its event ("Routine Invoices stopped, Mail no longer allows it"), the name a bot saved for the operator. Tapping one shows the rest: the teammate conversation, the routine, or the routines to pick from.
- A turn under way with a plan shows it as a short card under its row, and its little ones in a column under the bot's face; a room shows both under each member at work.
- A finished turn's work folds into one line that says how it ended and how many steps it took, with no time ("Worked · 6 steps", "Stopped · 2 steps", "Failed · 1 step"), and opens to show the steps, each with its mark: done, failed, or running while the turn still runs. Work the bot left going after its reply reads as part of that finished turn: its steps are among the fold's, said as done ("Ran a command"), with no line or live mark of their own. Little ones it left working are the exception: the row keeps the bot's face and "Working" over them until the last is home, and only then folds. The turn's plan folds into a line of its own ("Plan done, 4 of 4") that opens the plan. A step where the bot looked at an image shows it small beside the step; a tap opens it large. It shows nowhere else.
- A connector's tool can come with its own interface, such as a table of what a shop has in stock: it shows under the turn's work line, open or folded, in a card that names the tool and its connector ("Stock · Wheelhouse"), and the operator uses it in place. What it asks of the operator waits under it in J3 Bots' own words: running one of its connector's tools as the bot (Allow, Don't allow), once per tool, unless the bot is in Full access; sending the bot a message as theirs (Send, Not now); opening a link in the browser (Open, Not now). Nothing it does reaches the bot unless the operator sends it.
- While the bot has a page open in its browser, a globe in the header, and a picture of the page under the turn under way, open the browser.
- A composer labeled with the bot's name, or the room's ("Message Support"). Attachments and dictation are in the composer; nothing else is. A file stands in the message where the operator put it ("compare [image] with [image]"), and the bot reads it there, numbered and named as in the harness's list of files. A tap on a file shows it: an image in a viewer, anything else in the app that opens it.

### Details

Answers: what is this bot, and what runs without me.

- Profile: name, title, avatar, job, with Share as template.
- Routines with their schedule (or the event they run on), next run, last result, and a pause switch; one on an event says when it waits for its connector or stopped, and why; a routine in steps shows its bots' faces and its sentence.
- Files the bot produced.
- Memory, readable and editable.
- Connectors this bot may use, each with its status, and a way to the connectors screen. With none, one line says to ask the bot to add one.
- Teammates: the teammate conversation with each bot it wrote or heard from, with the last message and its time, a deleted teammate's included.
- Model and permission mode.

### Outside those three

- **Command palette** on desktop and web: jump to a bot, a room or a message; run any action by name.
- **Quick access**, in the desktop app: a small card on the primary bot's conversation, which the global shortcut opens over whatever the operator is doing ([A quick word with the primary bot](#a-quick-word-with-the-primary-bot)).
- **Settings**: eleven pages, each holding what an operator looks for in one place, in three groups: **You** (name and time zone), **Notifications**, **Appearance** (theme and language, this device's), **Voice** (dictation, where neither the server nor the device can dictate left out); **Providers**, **Usage**, **MCP and skills** (the connectors and the skills), **Secrets** (with the bots' browser and its sign-ins); **Devices** (this device, every paired one, pairing another, and signing out), **Where your bots live** (in the desktop app and on a phone) and **About and updates**. On a wide window the list stays beside the page; on a narrow one and on a phone, the list opens each page, and Back returns to it. Each page says in one line what it holds; what cannot be undone stands apart at its end. A page that waits on the operator carries a dot in the list: providers that cannot run a bot, a connector that needs them, an update on offer, and in the desktop app this device's sign-in stored without encryption. Providers shows the engine and each provider's status, each provider that cannot run with why, the engine's own words when it gave any, and the same rows as the first run, one per AI account with its action beside its status; under an account, once it is connected, its harness's version, and, when a newer one is out, **Update** where the engine can install it, or that the operator updates it the way they installed it; while an update runs, Settings says so, and it says how it ended. A harness J3 Bots installed itself shows the same line, with one **Update** that J3 Bots runs: its progress and **Cancel** while it downloads and checks, that it waits for the bots using it to finish what they are doing before it switches, and how the last install or update ended; and one switch, on unless turned off, that has J3 Bots keep those copies up to date by itself. About and updates shows the server's version and channel, and in the desktop app and on a phone, the app's. Under them, **Privacy** holds together what leaves the server about how it runs, each where the server offers it. First the server's switch for anonymous usage data, on unless turned off, with one sentence of what it is, a link to what is sent and what never is, and "See what is sent", which lists the events the server sent as they left ([Usage data](usage-data.md)). Then **Download diagnostics**, for when something behaves oddly and the operator wants help. It opens one small sheet, which:

- says the file is for whoever helps them;
- lists what is inside: the logs of the last 7 days, failures and updates, versions, settings and counts, and whether usage data is on with what it last sent;
- lists what never is: the operator's messages, names of bots, rooms or people, and passwords, keys or other secrets;
- offers Cancel and Save (Share on a phone).

Save downloads the file; on a phone, Share hands it to the system's share sheet. While the file is made the sheet says so and stays open; when it fails, the sheet says so and Save tries again. There is nothing to choose: what is safe to pass on is settled by J3 Bots, not by the operator. The row shows only where the server can make the file.
- **Updates on the phone.** When the server or the app has an update, the update dialog opens over the roster, as on the other clients, and "Update now" updates the server first and the app after it. After "Later", one quiet line over the roster says an update waits and opens the dialog again. While the server updates, the same line shows how far it has come, its restart included, in place of the line that says the server cannot be reached. It then says once how the update ended. The app updates itself as it opens: with "Update automatically" on, the default, it downloads a newer release, checks it against the project's signature and installs it with no tap, while the same line says how far it has come and that the app closes to finish; Android installs it without asking where it allows that, and asks once otherwise. An update that does not go through says so in that line, once, and the app tries again the next time it opens. Turned off in Settings → About and updates, the app's update is offered in the dialog, and Android asks to install it. The first time, the app explains Android's "Install unknown apps" before it opens that page, and goes on when the operator comes back. A phone whose server speaks another protocol shows the side that is behind, with the update that brings it forward. A server behind the app is first asked to look for releases, so the phone says none is out yet only after it has looked. An app behind its server looks for its own release as the screen opens, and has its check and its channel there, since the server may run a nightly.

## States the interface must always show

A bot is in exactly one of these, and the avatar carries it:

| State | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Idle | Nothing running |
| Thinking | A turn started and no visible work has happened yet |
| Working | A turn is running tools |
| Waiting | The bot asked a question or needs an approval |
| Blocked | The turn cannot continue: provider limit, error, engine unreachable |
| Done | Idle, with a result the operator has not read. It follows from the unread mark, so reading the result makes the bot idle on every device |

While a first contact's next message is a moment from arriving, the line that says what the bot is doing says "Typing", in its row and in its conversation; the avatar stays Thinking or Working.

"Working" and "stuck" must never look the same. A bot whose turn has produced no activity for a long time is shown as possibly stalled ("No activity for a while", with no time), with a way to check on it or stop it.

When its AI is hard to reach and the engine is trying again, the line says so quietly: "Having trouble reaching Claude, trying again". A retry that works leaves no trace.

**Errors say what happened and what to do, in plain words.** Only what a person can understand or act on is shown: a usage limit and when it resets, the AI's account signed out (sign in again in Settings), the AI unreachable (check the internet connection), or work that couldn't finish (try again). The AI is named when the bot is known. Nothing on screen names the engine, a provider's error class or a code. The technical words, scrubbed of secrets, sit behind **Details** on the notice, for whoever wants them, and in the server's log.

**What the engine says shows only when it asks something of the operator or explains a surprise.** The engine running the bots notes many things as they work. One is shown: a bot that works in Auto or Full access but whose AI asks before acting anyway, because Auto isn't available to it with that account or model, or because its settings on the computer don't let it skip asking. The bot's conversation says so once, where the approvals will appear, with what to do: answer as the questions come, or choose another model or permission mode, or ask whoever manages the AI's settings. Everything else the engine notes stays in the server's log. The bot keeps its magic: nothing shows its working context being compacted, or the work that goes on after its turn ([decision 0083](../decisions/0083-only-the-engine-notices-a-person-can-act-on-show.md)).
