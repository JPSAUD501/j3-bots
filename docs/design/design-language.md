# Design language

**Status:** every screen follows the Pips direction ([decision 0018](../decisions/0018-the-interface-follows-pips.md)) on the web, the desktop app, which draws the web build, and Android, the conversation and the first run included, and so do the [avatars](#avatars) and the [bot palette](#bot-palette). The values below are the ones in [`packages/design-tokens`](../../packages/design-tokens/src/tokens.ts), and the contrast figures are checked by its tests. The web client's development build has a gallery at `/gallery` that shows every component in every state, in both themes, with the springs to replay; it is the place to review the design, and it is not part of a production build. Nothing of it has run on iOS or iPadOS yet.

## Two references, one product

J3 Bots' interface is a deliberate mix of two existing designs. Both are described here in words; neither's assets are reproduced.

**From persistent-agent products (xAI's Grok Bot is the clearest example):** the interface of a messaging app, organized around who you are talking to.

- The sidebar is a roster of named teammates, not a history of chats. Each row is an avatar, a name, a time, and one line that reads like a status update from a person.
- The avatar is the presence indicator. It changes with what the bot is doing, so there is no separate spinner or status badge. The one mark an avatar can carry is the primary bot's, which names a role, not a state.
- Conversations are bubbles. The operator's are dark and right-aligned, the bot's are light and left-aligned, grouped, with timestamps between groups.
- The chrome is almost monochrome. Color belongs to the bots.
- Structured answers arrive as cards in the same timeline as prose.
- A side pane shows what the bot is and what runs without you: its routines.
- Controls that do not help the operator delegate are removed.

**From T3 Code:** the precision and density of a developer tool.

- A true-black dark canvas and near-white light canvas, zinc neutrals, hairline borders, small radii.
- A compact type scale where a mouse leads: 15 px messages, 12 px metadata.
- A finished turn's work folds into one line ("Worked · 6 steps") that opens to show each step.
- A command palette that reaches every action, and a shortcut for everything.
- Semantic color tokens as CSS variables with a light and a dark value each.
- Components in a `ui` layer own their look; callers choose a variant.

**The synthesis.** J3 Bots looks like a messaging app and behaves like a professional tool. Messaging gives the structure: roster, bubbles, presence. The developer tool gives the manner: density, keyboard reach, and work that is always inspectable but never in the way.

**Pips.** The bots are where the life is: small characters that look at the operator and show what they are doing with their bodies, on chrome that stays quiet, near-monochrome and precise. Motion is quick and springy, and it serves the bots and the operator's hands, never decoration ([decision 0018](../decisions/0018-the-interface-follows-pips.md)). How they move follows the Palco direction ([decision 0052](../decisions/0052-motion-follows-palco.md)): a stage where what was just made comes down into a light and lands with weight, and motion that carries on from wherever it is when something interrupts it.

## Principles

1. **Who before what.** Every screen starts from a bot. There is no screen that lists tasks, runs or sessions as first-class things.
2. **One line of truth.** Each roster row carries one line: what the bot last said, or what it is doing now, or what it needs. The row that needs the operator is visibly different from the rest.
3. **Work is available, not present.** The steps of a turn are one tap away and never open by themselves.
4. **Color means someone or something.** A color on screen is a bot's identity or a state (waiting, blocked, error). Decoration does not use color.
5. **Lively bots, quiet chrome.** The bots move; the chrome around them answers the operator's hand and then keeps still. Nothing moves that reduced motion would not stop.
6. **Same words everywhere.** A bot, a room, a routine, a card: the same nouns on every surface and in every prompt.

## Layout

Three regions, the same on every surface.

| Region | Wide (1200 px and up) | Tablet (768 to 1199 px) | Phone (below 768 px) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Roster | Left column, 280 px | Left column, 280 px | First screen |
| Conversation | Center, text column capped at 720 px | Center | Pushed screen |
| Details | Right pane, 320 px, closed by default, beside the conversation | Modal dialog over the conversation, with a scrim that closes it | Modal dialog that covers the screen |
| The bot's browser | In the details' place, as wide as the conversation can spare: 400 to 960 px, leaving it at least 448 px | Modal dialog over the whole conversation, with a scrim | Sheet rising from the bottom over a scrim, the conversation's top still in sight |

- The layout follows the width of the interface's own container, not the window's, so the same component lays out correctly in a narrow frame on a wide screen.
- The conversation text column has a maximum width; on a wide window the space goes to margins and the details pane, not to longer lines.
- The details pane opens from the bot's name in the conversation header, or from the pane button beside it. On a phone the name carries a chevron, because there is no pane button. At the wide layout the conversation gives up the pane's width at once and the pane slides in from the end edge into it; closing, the pane slides out and the conversation takes the width back once it has gone. As a dialog it slides in from the end edge over its scrim, the left edge in a right-to-left language. Either way the details keep their own width while they move, so nothing in them reflows.
- Below the wide layout the details pane is a modal dialog: the roster and the conversation behind it are inert, focus is trapped inside it, the dialog handles `Escape`, and the conversation's shortcuts wait until it closes. At the wide layout it sits beside the conversation, nothing else is blocked, and `Escape` closes it from anywhere in the shell.
- The bot's browser and the details take the same place: opening one closes the other. Leaving the browser while the operator holds the page, by any of its ways out (its close button, `Escape`, the scrim, the details' button), asks first whether to give it back.
- On phones every region keeps clear of the screen's safe areas (the notch, rounded corners and home indicator) on the edges it touches, in portrait and landscape.

### Keyboard and focus

| Action | Keys |
| --- | --- |
| Next and previous bot or room, in the order the roster draws them (pinned bots, the list, then each section; bots and rooms behind the scenes and those in a folded section are skipped); with none open, the first or the last | `Alt+↓`, `Alt+↑` (`⌥↓`, `⌥↑` on macOS) |
| Put the cursor in the composer | `/` |
| Stop the bot that is working, or whose background work holds a change of its settings | `Ctrl+.` (`⌘.` on macOS) |
| Close the details pane, or the bot's browser, before anything else | `Escape` |
| Take the keyboard off the bot's page, to Done | `F6` |
| Move through the roster | `↓` from the search field, then `↑`, `↓`, `Home`, `End` |

- Shortcut hints are shown where the action is: on the Stop row in the bot's details, and on the empty screen for the next-bot shortcut. Touch screens show neither. Only a coarse pointer hides anything: with no pointer at all, as with a keyboard alone or a TV remote, every hint and the inline "Waiting for you" stay.
- The roster's arrow keys are unmodified ones; `Alt+↑` and `Alt+↓` pass through it to the shell.
- Opening the details pane moves focus to its heading; closing it on the same conversation, by any route, returns focus to the conversation title. Choosing another conversation with a mouse or keyboard puts the cursor in its composer; on touch screens it does not, so the keyboard does not cover the conversation. Focus never moves under a dialog: one left open over a conversation a notification opened keeps it. When the details close because another conversation was chosen, the move wins and focus follows it. Loading a page moves focus nowhere.
- Each conversation opens at its own newest entry, never where the previous one was scrolled to.
- While the operator holds the bot's page, every key typed with the page focused goes to the page, `Escape` and the app's shortcuts included, and nothing else answers it. `F6` takes the focus back to Done, from where `Tab` and `Escape` work as anywhere; a fine pointer shows that hint beside Done. Taking control puts the focus on the page; Done moves it nowhere.
- A command palette (`Cmd/Ctrl+K`) that reaches every action is planned and not built yet.

## Color

Near-monochrome neutrals carry the interface, on a true-black dark canvas. The focus ring, links, unread and the primary action are the text color, so the chrome adds no hue of its own. Semantic colors mark state. A separate palette belongs to bots.

Elevation is for what lies over other content. `raised` is a faint shadow under a card in light and none in dark; `overlay`, under popovers, menus, dialogs and sheets, is a soft drop shadow in light and, since no shadow shows on black, a darker one with a hairline of white at 7% in dark; `thumb` lifts a switch's thumb off its track; `lift` is under a roster row while it travels to a new place, a drop shadow in light and, in dark, a deeper one of black at 90% with a hairline of white at 9%, written in `rgba()` so Android's `boxShadow` draws the same. Tailwind's own shadow scale is removed, so only these exist, and a web test fails on a shadow class outside them.

Destructive actions use `blocked`: there is no separate destructive red. Text is never drawn with a text token at reduced opacity, since that would put it below the measured contrast; the one exception is a disabled control (see [Accessibility](#accessibility)).

### Bot palette

Each bot has one color, chosen by the operator from twelve: tomato, tangerine, honey, lime, mint, teal, sky, cobalt, iris, orchid, rose and slate. They run round the hue circle, and slate is the one quiet color. Each has a light and a dark value, kept apart from the interface tokens in `packages/design-tokens/src/botPalette.ts`. The operator, or a bot asked to change its avatar, can choose only these names, and the server refuses any other; a client draws slate for a color it does not know.

The light values sit darker than the dark ones so that a body reads as a shape on every canvas. What the palette guarantees, and what the tests check:

- every body reaches 3:1 against `background`, `surface`, `sidebar` and `muted` in its theme (measured minimum 3.19 in light, mint on `muted`, and 3.86 in dark, cobalt on `muted`), and against `avatar-ink`, white in light and near-black in dark, which the interface draws over a bot's color (minimum 3.63, mint, and 4.33, cobalt);
- every pair of bodies stays at least 0.085 apart in OKLab for typical vision (measured minimum 0.092 in both themes, honey and lime in light, teal and sky in dark).

Twelve saturated hues cannot all stay apart under a color vision deficiency: simulated after Machado, Oliveira and Fernandes (2009) at full severity, protanopia and deuteranopia bring the warm hues together, and cobalt and iris, to within 0.02. The silhouette carries the difference instead: the cast gives the first twelve bots twelve different shapes, and color is never the only thing that tells two bots apart.

The eye is the same in both themes: a white lens and a near-black pupil (`#141418`), 18.4:1 apart. On the palest bodies in dark (honey, lime) the lens stands only 1.7:1 from the body, so the eye reads by its pupil. An offline bot's body keeps 12% of its saturation, the web's `saturate(0.12)`, and is drawn at half opacity.

A bot's bubbles take a tint of its color: its palette value mixed over the canvas in OKLab, 11% in light and 28% in dark, where a lighter tint would vanish, with its chroma reduced where the mix leaves sRGB, as light tomato's does (`botBubble`, beside the palette in `botPalette.ts`, emitted as `bot-bubble-{color}`); the tests hold the text and code in them to 4.5:1 for every color in both themes. A bot's mention is a chip of the same tint with the bubble's bot text over it, so it reads at that contrast wherever it stands: in the operator's bubble, in another bot's, or in the composer. Each client reads a bot's tint and color through one helper (`botTint`), so where a bot's color comes from can change in one place.

## Avatars

The avatar is the most important element on screen: it is how the operator recognizes a bot at a glance and how they read its state.

Requirements:

- Recognizable at 24 px in a list and expressive at 96 px in a profile.
- Built from two choices the operator makes, a **shape** and a **color**, so that a roster of twelve bots has twelve distinct silhouettes.
- Carries every state through its own form, with motion only as an addition.
- Works as a still image for notifications and for reduced-motion settings.

An avatar is a **Pip**: a small character whose body is the bot's silhouette in its color, with one lens eye and an antenna. The body is the bot's identity; the eye and the antenna carry its state. The antenna's tip is the status light, so no badge and no spinner carry state. The one corner mark is the [primary bot's](#primary-mark), which says who the bot is, never what it is doing. The design is J3 Bots' own: no other product's characters, art or animation are reproduced.

**Shapes.** Twelve, drawn in a 100-unit box (`packages/design-tokens/src/avatarShapes.ts`) and listed in this order: dome, block, bean, loaf, drop, ears, horns, ghost, hex, gem, toes and pear. Each is an outline in absolute path commands only, so the PNG renderer draws exactly what the SVG renderers do, stroked in its own color to round its corners (pointed shapes take a thicker stroke). Every body stands on the same line, its eye and antenna root where it is widest and highest, and a renderer frames the same square for all of them (`avatarViewBox`), so bots of different shapes line up in a row.

**The cast.** The server gives a bot created without an avatar the pair of a shape and a color that the bots use least, and the pickers offer that same pair first (`freeAvatar` in `@j3-bots/design-tokens/names`). Of all 144 pairs it takes the one the fewest bots have as a pair, then the one whose shape and color the fewest bots have, then the first in order: the pairs of `avatarCast` first, then the rest by the cast's order of their shape and then of their color. The cast names each of the twelve shapes and twelve colors once, ordered so neighbors differ in hue, lightness and outline: a tangerine dome, a cobalt ears, a mint drop, an orchid horns, a honey ghost, an iris block, and so on. The first twelve bots therefore differ in both shape and color, no two bots share a pair until all 144 are taken, and a shape or color the operator chose for one bot is passed over for the next. The first run's stage and the screens before it stand five of the first six on their floor, the cast's first in the middle, which is also the look the first bot starts with; the screens while the web client connects, and its wide layout with no conversation open, show the first three as the ensemble.

**States.** What an avatar shows comes from `avatarMood` in `packages/client-runtime`: what concerns the operator first, then a turn under way, then news.

| State | When | Eye | Antenna and light | Signal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Idle | Nothing running | Open, resting | Upright, the body's color | None |
| Thinking | A turn started, no visible work yet | Lid lowered, looking up and aside | Leans back | Three dots |
| Working | A turn is running tools | Narrowed on the work | Leans forward | A turning ring |
| Waiting | The bot asked a question or needs an approval | Wide, looking at the operator | Tall, light `waiting` | A ping from the light |
| Blocked | The turn cannot continue | Shut, tilted | Drooping, light `blocked` | None |
| Unread | Idle, with something the operator has not read | Wide, smiling, with cheeks | Light `foreground`, larger | None |
| Speaking | A message of the bot's is arriving | Open, lifted, the mouth moving | Upright | Sound arcs |
| Offline | This client has not seen the server for 3 s | Shut | Drooping, short | None; the body is washed out and drawn at half opacity |

- A bot that waits on the operator or is blocked shows it even while it speaks. A notification's still avatar shows what the notification means: waiting for a card, unread for a message (`notificationState`).
- Speaking lasts from 0.9 to 2.8 s after the message's own time, longer for a longer message, so it plays while the conversation brings the words in and never again on a reload.
- A turn the server reports as possibly stalled holds its state's still form without its signal: a stuck bot stops moving and nothing at its light says the work goes on, so working and stuck never look the same, under reduced motion too.
- The light's three colors match the roster line's wording and color, and each state also changes the eye and the antenna, so state is never carried by color alone.
- A light in a state's color sits in a band of what the avatar is on, the way a room's front member is cut out of the one behind, so it stands clear of the body and the stalk whatever the bot's color, even where a blocked bot's antenna droops over its body (`lightCut`). A light in the body's color has no band: it is the antenna's tip, so an idle tomato or honey bot's tip never reads as a blocked or waiting light. The band takes the color of what the avatar is on at the moment, so it turns `muted` with a roster row or the conversation header while it is hovered, pressed or selected.
- Every state has a still form (`stillFace`): its pose held and its signal as one frame. It is what reduced motion shows, what notifications and the tray draw, and what small inline avatars (bubbles, the composer's mentions, cards, usage) use.
- Avatars of 28 px and under draw the eye, the stalk and the light larger so they stay legible.
- A room's avatar overlaps the bodies of its first two members, which sleep with every other bot while this client is offline.
- In a conversation's header, a room shows its members' still avatars overlapped, three at most, and "+2" for the rest; a teammate conversation shows its two bots overlapped, as a room's own avatar is drawn.

**Life.** Each bot moves in its own way, from a seed hashed from its id (`avatarSeed`), the same on every device: how it leans its antenna, how fast it breathes, where its eye rests, when it blinks and glances, which idle moves it is fondest of.

- **One place it moves from.** Every part has one channel in the model, and nothing else writes it: the body's height, its squash and stretch, its lean, the antenna's lag, the eye's gaze, its pupil and lids, the light. On the ground the body rides `pop`, the lean `soft`, the antenna's tip and the light `pop`, the gaze `slide` (the interface's [springs](#motion)); off the ground the body falls as a thrown thing does, at one gravity for every size (1100 of the avatar's 100 units per second squared), so a hop of 9 units hangs a quarter of a second.
- **Acts.** What a bot does besides holding its pose is an act: a few beats, each a set of targets the channels travel toward (a duck, a throw, a lean, a smile, a look). A new act replaces the one under way and the channels go on from where they are, at the speed they have; a throw sets the body's speed and never adds to it, less the higher the body already is. So nothing resets a bot, and ten taps in a second rise no higher than a few hops' worth.
- **Idle.** It breathes, shifts its weight a little on two slow waves, blinks (one blink in five is two), glances, and every 5 to 14 s does something small: looks around, stretches, flicks its antenna, tilts its head, bounces. Half the time it is its favorite. The antenna's tip trails the body: behind a lean, below a rise, above a fall, and whips through as the body stops.
- **The pointer.** The eye follows the pointer only from close by, in the states where the bot looks out at the operator (idle, waiting, unread, speaking): with all its attention within 2 of the avatar's own widths from its centre, none past 3, easing between the two, and easing over time as well, so it never snaps (`pointerNear`, `avatarNearness`). An avatar under 36 px counts as 36 wide, so the small ones in a list are not blind. The eye turns fully toward a pointer 0.8 widths away or more and looks straight out at one right over it; close by, the body and the antenna lean a little its way, and within 0.9 widths the pupil widens. A pointer that has lain still for 3.5 s is let go. On a touch screen a finger is the pointer while it is down.
- **A press and a tap.** The pointer coming over a bot at rest makes it notice: it stretches, its pupil widens, its antenna perks. A press holds it down, squashed; letting go lets it spring up; a tap gets one of three answers, never the one before: a hop with a happy squint, a giggle that rocks it side to side, or a deep squash and a small pop. A bot blocked or asleep does not play, and neither does an avatar drawn still, as beside a message or in a room's pair of faces: those answer nothing, and move only to come in when they are new.
- **A change of state** eases the face into the new pose and starts an act in the state's own way: thinking tips its head, working ducks to it, waiting and unread hop, blocked slumps, and a bot back to idle blinks. A bot that waits hops again every 2.6 to 4.8 s, and one with news every 2 to 3.8 s.
- **An entrance.** A bot new to the roster, and the first run's cast, come in from above: twice their own height up, or 0.9 of it at list size so a bot never falls through the row above its own, falling, stretched the way they travel, landing with a squash as deep as they came down fast, then a small rebound; the antenna's light is dim until the landing and comes on with a halo, and the bot looks up, pleased. Nothing plays for an avatar that merely appears, as when a roster loads.
- The picker's preview and the first run's bot answer a new name, shape or color the same way: a key typed makes the bot look down and bob, a new shape is taken in a squash, a new color is a hop.

**Rendering.** The model is pure and shared (`packages/client-runtime/src/presentation/avatar.ts`): a renderer steps it once a frame and draws the face through `avatarFrame` from the tokens.

- **Web:** one SVG per avatar and one frame loop for the page (`ticker.ts`), which also steps whatever else travels on a spring it can be interrupted on (`lib/sprung.ts`: the first run's camera, the roster's rows). A frame writes only the avatar's own SVG attributes, transforms for the body and the eye and the paths that bend (the antenna, the lids, the mouth), which repaint the avatar without laying out the page, and writes one only when its value changed. It reads layout, the avatars' boxes, only while a pointer that moved lately may be near one, only after the page scrolled or something travelled or half a second after it last read them, since a pointer that moves does not move an avatar, and always before it writes, so drawing never forces a layout. A frame counts for a tenth of a second at most, so a tab that slept does not fast-forward. In headless Chromium, on the development build with software rendering, a roster of 40 idle bots alone on the page, 24 of them moving and the pointer wandering over them, drew 59.5 frames a second, 3 frames in 603 later than 20 ms and none later than 67 ms, for 64 ms of script and 7 ms of layout in each second, against 50 ms and 1 ms with every avatar still; four entrances into the roster, one of them landing while rows were still on their way, and one leaving, drew 57.6, with 15 frames in 590 later than 20 ms; the first run played from the welcome to the conversation drew a frame every 16.7 ms at the median and at the 95th percentile, 31 in 1025 later than 20 ms, the longest as the conversation mounted (**Verified**, on a laptop busy with other work; the pure model takes 12 µs to step an avatar and lay its frame out). An avatar out of view (IntersectionObserver) is not stepped, nor one asleep, which rests and would otherwise filter its washed-out body again each frame; one under 44 px steps every other frame, and at most 24 move at once: a bot asked to answer something within the last second and a half and a [family of little ones](#little-ones) first, then the largest. The others hold their still form, and draw the new one when their state changes.
- **Android:** `react-native-svg`, with each avatar stepped on the UI thread by a Reanimated frame callback, which also reads where the finger is (`finger.ts`, written from the app's outermost view) and measures the avatar only while a finger is down. The JS side decides whose callbacks run: none while the app is in the background, none off screen or on a screen out of focus, in the roster only the rows the list reports in view and none while it scrolls (each holds its pose until the list settles), under 44 px at half rate, and at most 24 at once, a family of little ones first, then the largest. A frame sets an SVG prop only when its value changed, and values are rounded to a quarter pixel, so a part that moved less than that is not drawn again. On the Pixel 8 emulator, with its software GPU and a release build, a roster of 20 bots drew 1% of its frames late at rest and 12% while scrolling, against 5% with every avatar still (**Verified**, before the body's springs moved to `pop` and `soft`; not measured since, and not with the motion of [decision 0052](../decisions/0052-motion-follows-palco.md), which has not run on a device).
- **PNG:** the rasterizer in `packages/design-tokens/src/raster` draws the still forms for notifications and the tray.

**Image avatars** are not available yet: the contract's avatar is a shape and a color only.

### Primary mark

The primary bot ([Concepts](../product/concepts.md)) carries a mark at its avatar's lower end corner wherever its avatar names it: a roster row, a pinned bot and the conversation header, and on Android the details too. The details also keep a "Primary" badge beside the name. Small inline avatars, beside a message, in a mention or in a turn's row, go without it. The mark names a role, so it never changes with the bot's state, and the light still says what the bot is doing.

- A five-pointed star in `accent-foreground` on a disc of `accent`, the text color, so it reads as the chrome's own sign and takes no bot's color. The disc is 34% of the avatar's size and never under 14 px, so the star still reads on a phone's 28 px header avatar.
- A band of what the avatar sits on surrounds the disc, 2 px wide, 3 px from a 56 px avatar up, cutting it out of the body the way a state's light is cut out ([`lightCut`](#avatars)). The band, not the disc's color, is what keeps it apart from the body, so it reads on all twelve colors in both themes, on a near-black slate in light and a near-white honey in dark alike. Like the light's band it takes the color of what the avatar is on at the moment, `muted` while a row is pressed or selected.
- Its center sits 16% of the avatar's size in from the corner, so it overlaps the body's edge. The antenna rises from the body's top and the eye sits high, so the corner never covers either.
- Screen readers hear it as "Primary bot" in the name of what holds the avatar, never as an image of its own.
- **On Android** it is drawn with the avatar (`PrimaryMark`, around `LiveBotAvatar`).

### Little ones

While part of a bot's turn runs in parallel, the bot splits into **little ones**: small copies of itself, one for each piece of work, which stand under its face, work, and go back into it ([decision 0071](../decisions/0071-a-bot-splits-into-little-ones-under-its-face-and-its-plan-is-a-quiet-card.md)). A little one is a part of the bot, never a teammate: it has no name, no state light and no conversation.

- **Its look.** The bot's own silhouette at three quarters of the bot's size beside the turn (18 px under a 24 px face). Its body is the bot's color made lighter, 46% of it over white in the light theme and 58% in the dark (`botLittle`), with a rim of the color itself 5 units wider than the body's outline, which is what keeps its silhouette 3:1 against every canvas. Its eye is 1.16 times the bot's and set 3 units lower, with a larger glint, as a young face has; where the antenna is it has a sprout 8 units long with a round tip in the rim's color (`littleFace`). It is drawn by the same frame as an avatar, told it is a little one (`avatarFrame`).
- **Where.** In a column under the bot's face, each on a line of its own, with one line of what it does beside it, in the turn's own text size, cut to one line. Four at most (the turn's work says which, [0070](../decisions/0070-a-turns-plan-and-its-little-ones-are-its-work.md)); a last line counts the rest, "+2 more", beside two specks in the little ones' colors. A line is 28 px tall on the web and 36 px on Android, where it is pressed.
- **Its face.** At its line its eye is a little narrowed on its words, and it bobs on its own beat, each one out of step with the others. As it lands it looks up at the bot; when a brother goes home it looks up after it; the pointer over it, or a finger on it, makes it perk up.
- **Failed.** Its body and rim lose their color (`littleFailed`, greys that keep the rim 3:1 against the canvases and the body 3:1 under the eye's white), its eye shuts into a droop and its sprout falls. Its words turn `muted-foreground`.
- **Its details.** A hover or a press on the line shows a small card over it: the line in full, and "Couldn't finish" for one that failed; never how long it has worked, as nothing of a turn under way counts the time. On Android a tap opens it and a press anywhere closes it.
- **After the reply.** Little ones a turn handed off stay under the bot's face once the turn is over, until each is home; the row keeps the face, with "Working" for its line, and folds into its own once the last is home ([decision 0095](../decisions/0095-little-ones-a-turn-handed-off-stay-until-they-are-home.md)).
- **In a room** the same family stands under the face and name of each member whose turn there has a plan or little ones, above the line that says who is answering.
- **In the roster** a bot that is split carries one dot for each little one at work by its avatar, its ended turns' included, four at most, along its lower edge on the side away from the primary bot's star (`littleDots`): the little ones' color, 9 px, in a band of what the row is on. Screen readers hear how many ("3 little ones working").

The family moves from one model (`packages/client-runtime/src/presentation/littleOnes.ts`), as an avatar does from its own: [Motion](#motion) says how.

### App icons

Every platform's icon is one brand mark: "j3" as a lettermark in one weight, white on the chrome's near-black, where the dot of the j is the status light every bot carries on its antenna ([decision 0076](../decisions/0076-the-brand-mark-is-j3-with-a-bots-light.md)). The j is a stem with a hook and its light stands clear above it; the 3 is two open bowls, the lower a little wider. The only color is the light's, the iris of the bot palette, with a soft glow around it on a dark ground. The plate is shaded from a lighter near-black at its top to a deeper one at its bottom, and it carries the mark on a light wallpaper and a dark one alike. The bots' own avatars are Pips and stay so: the icon is the product, and a Pip is a bot.

`packages/design-tokens/src/appIconArt.ts` holds it: the letters as path data, the light, the glow, the plate's colors, and one drawing for each range of sizes. A small icon is not the large one shrunk. From 64 px up the letters are drawn at 0.86 of their size on a 100-unit plate, so they span a little over half its width, with strokes about 9 of its units wide and the glow. At 48 px they are drawn at 0.9, strokes about 10 units, with no glow. At 32, 24 and 16 px they are drawn at 0.94, 0.97 and their full size, with strokes about 11.3, 12.6 and 14.5 units, the plate is one flat color, and the light is a size larger and a step brighter, so at a few px it stays a dot of color and not a gray one. `raster/appIcon.ts` draws every icon from it with the repository's own rasterizer, which fills a color or a linear or radial gradient (the plate's shading and the glow). `pnpm --filter @j3-bots/design-tokens generate` writes every native file from it to `packages/design-tokens/generated/icons`, and a test fails when they are stale:

- **Desktop:** the mark on a rounded plate, from 16 to 1024 px for Linux and macOS, and in one icon file with each size Windows draws, from 16 to 256 px, each in the drawing of its size. Below 64 px the plate fills the image, and from 64 px up it is inset by a sixteenth; macOS gets its own plate, inset as its icons are. The tray shows the mark alone, without a plate, in the theme the system draws its tray in, in the drawing of its size on screen. While anything needs the operator its light takes the waiting color, half as large again, with the letters cleared around it, so the change shows in the light's size and shape and not only its color, since on Linux and Windows no count stands beside the tray.
- **Android:** an adaptive icon of two 108 dp layers: the shaded plate as the background, and the mark as the foreground, placed as on a plate 86 dp square, so the letters and the light stay inside the 66 dp circle every mask (circle, squircle, rounded square) keeps, and the plate's shading runs past the mask for a launcher that moves the layers. The themed icon is the mark in the same place as a white silhouette, without the glow, which the launcher tints. The splash shows the mark alone on each theme's canvas, in that theme's colors. A notification's icon is the mark as a white silhouette on transparency, 96 px, since Android draws it from its alpha only and the opaque launcher icon would show as a white square; it is the large drawing, the one whose light stands clear of the j's stem, which in one color is all that keeps them two shapes, and the notification tints it with the light's iris, which reads on a light shade and a dark one where the chrome's near-black accent would not.
- **iOS:** the mark on a square plate, which iOS rounds; the mark alone in its colors on no background for the dark icon, and as a silhouette for the tinted one.
- **Web:** the same icons, drawn as the web client builds. The favicon is an icon file with each size from 16 to 64 px, each in the drawing of its size on its rounded plate, so it reads on a light tab strip and a dark one alike. The maskable icon and the one iOS adds to the Home Screen are the plate over the whole image, the mark inside the middle 80% circle a maskable icon is sure of.

A notification about a bot shows that bot's still avatar, not the mark: it says who wrote. The mark stands in where there is no avatar to draw, and on Android's status bar, which takes one icon for the app.

## Typography

Three faces, bundled with every client under the SIL Open Font License, so the app works offline and loads nothing from a font service ([the fonts and how they were cut](../../packages/design-tokens/fonts/README.md)):

| Face | Use | Weights |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Bricolage Grotesque** | Display: page and dialog titles, the gallery, a bot's name on its profile, the first run. Set tight, at −0.035 em | 700 |
| **Instrument Sans** | Everything that is read: messages, rows, controls, metadata | 400, 500, 600 |
| **JetBrains Mono** | Code, commands, identifiers and the steps of a turn, without ligatures, so `!=` and `->` read as typed | 400 |

- The files hold Latin, Latin-1, Latin Extended-A, general punctuation and arrows. A character outside them, as in a bot's name in another script, is drawn in the platform's font.
- On the web the faces are `@font-face` rules in the tokens' stylesheet, loaded from the bundle with the platform's font shown until they arrive. On Android they are embedded at build time, each family with its weights, so a weight picks its file; React Native has no inherited font for a `Text` on its own, so every `Text` and `TextInput` comes from the app's own `text.tsx`, which sets the face, and a lint rule refuses them from `react-native`. A `Text` nested in another sets no face unless it names one, so it inherits its parent's, as a line of a code block keeps the block's mono.
- **Weight:** text is regular (400) and medium (500); controls, a name that needs the operator, and buttons are semibold (600). Display is bold (700) only.
- Message text sets no line longer than about 70 characters.

### Type scale

The scale has five steps for text and four for headings, and each step has two sets of values: **touch**, for a phone read at arm's length, and **pointer**, for a screen where a mouse leads, read closer. Android always takes the touch set, in sp, so it follows the system's font size. The web takes the pointer set, in rem, and the touch set where its primary pointer is coarse (`@media (pointer: coarse)`), so the web on a phone reads as the Android app does. A screen names a step once (`text-sm`) and gets the right size on either; no screen sets a size of its own. Tailwind's own scale is removed, so only these steps exist.

| Step | Touch, size / line height | Pointer, size / line height | Used for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `2xs` | 13 / 18 | 12 / 16 | Times, statuses, the time between messages, the line that introduces a teammate's message |
| `xs` | 14 / 20 | 13 / 18 | Small labels, notices, code in a message; on the web, a roster row's line |
| `sm` | 16 / 22 | 14 / 20 | A roster row's line, hints; on the web, names, menu items and buttons |
| `base` | 17 / 22 | 15 / 22 | Names, menu items and a row's title; on the web, message text and the composer |
| `lg` | 17 / 24 | 16 / 24 | Message text and the composer on a touch screen |
| `xl` | 20 / 26 | 20 / 26 | Dialog and section titles |
| `2xl`, `3xl`, `4xl` | 24 / 28, 30 / 34, 40 / 42 | the same | The display face: a profile's name, a page's title, the first run |

**Why these sizes.** The scale was measured against the persistent-agent app this design takes its structure from, on one phone at one resolution, so pixels compared directly. Converted to Instrument Sans at the same x-height, that app sets message text, names, menu items, titles and the composer at about 17 sp, a list's second line at 16, a button at 14, and times and the lines between messages at 13. J3 Bots set them at 16, 14, 13 and 11. Both put about 24 sp between the lines of a message; the difference was the size of the letters, which made J3 Bots' names and metadata read small next to its messages. So:

- On touch, a name, a menu item and a message share one size, 17, and differ in leading: a paragraph needs 24 between its lines, a one-line name 22. Hierarchy comes from weight and color (a name is medium, a row's line is muted), not from shrinking the text under it.
- Nothing that is read is smaller than 13 on touch or 12 on a pointer screen. The 11 px step is gone: it was below what reads comfortably on a phone, and on a 1× desktop display.
- The pointer set keeps the developer tool's density: each step up to `base` is one pixel larger than before, with messages at 15 / 22, close to what chat apps on the desktop use, and the roster's names at 14.
- Instrument Sans has a tall x-height (0.51 em), so it reads about a size larger than a face of the same nominal size with a shorter one; the steps were chosen by x-height, not by nominal size.

**Font size settings.** Android's sizes are in sp, so the system's font size scales them, up to 200%. Line heights scale with them, so the lines of a paragraph never overlap. Messages, the line that introduces a teammate's message, the header and the composer grow with their text. The roster grows with its text too: each of its rows, headers and pinned bots is as tall as its lines need at the font size, and never shorter than at the default size, so at the largest sizes rows still have room between them; a name and a row's line end in an ellipsis rather than wrap. On the web the sizes are in rem and follow the browser's font size.

## Shape and space

- A 4 px spacing grid.
- Controls are 36 px tall on pointer devices and 44 px on touch screens; roster rows 56 and 64.
- Shapes are soft. Radii: 6 for badges, key caps, checkboxes and a bubble's tightened corner; 12 for fields, menu rows, chips of attached files and small tiles, such as a card's 28 px icon tile; 16 for roster rows, popovers, menus and cards; 20 for dialogs, bubbles and the cards in a conversation; 22 for the composer; 28 for the top corners of a sheet rising from a phone's bottom edge; and a pill for every button, switches and chips. The composer is a rounded rectangle at every height, never a pill, so text that wraps is never clipped by its corners.
- Consecutive bubbles from one speaker within five minutes form a group. On the speaker's side, the bottom corner of every bubble tightens to 6, and so does the top corner of each bubble after the first, so a group reads as one column with its tail at the end. A bubble on its own tightens only its bottom corner on the speaker's side. Bubbles in a group sit 4 px apart; a new group, or a card, starts 12 px below what came before. A pause of more than fifteen minutes puts a time between entries.
- Entry widths are layout tokens: a bubble takes up to 36 rem or 85% of the column, and a card up to 36 rem or 92%. On a phone a card that asks the operator for something (a question, an approval, a credential) takes the full column. The composer grows with its text up to 15 rem, then scrolls.
- Borders are hairlines. Cards in a conversation and the composer sit on `raised`; popovers, menus, dialogs, sheets and the button back to a conversation's end, which floats over it, on `overlay`.

## Motion

Quick and springy: things overshoot a little and settle fast. Every move runs on one of three springs, each named by its natural frequency and damping ratio, so every platform draws the same motion from the same two numbers ([`springs.ts`](../../packages/design-tokens/src/springs.ts)):

| Spring | Frequency, damping | Settles in | Overshoot | For |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `pop` | 3.6 Hz, 0.5 | 571 ms | 15% | A press springing back, a menu or a short dialog opening, a switch's thumb, something arriving |
| `soft` | 2.3 Hz, 0.72 | 562 ms | 3% | Something moving into place: a dialog's panel rising |
| `slide` | 2.5 Hz, 0.86 | 392 ms | under 1% | A pane or a sheet sliding, a fold opening |

- **Two ways to draw a spring.** What nothing interrupts, a dialog opening, a toast, a line of text arriving, plays the spring as a CSS `linear()` easing on the web: simulated once, sampled about 60 times a second, with the time it takes to settle as its duration (the utility `ease-pop` with `duration-(--duration-pop)`, and so on). What can be interrupted, an avatar, the first run's camera, a roster row on its way, is drawn from the spring's own solution (`springStep`), a frame at a time on the page's one frame loop: a new target bends the move from where it is and at the speed it has, where an easing would stop it and start again from rest. It is the solution, not a sum of small steps, so a frame of any length draws the same motion. **On Android** the same spring drives Reanimated as a physical spring of mass 1, stiffness ω² and damping 2ζω, so it has the same frequency and damping ratio, and Reanimated carries the speed over when it is retargeted.
- What has nothing to overshoot, a fade or a color, uses the `out` ease, `cubic-bezier(0.2, 0.8, 0.2, 1)`, over 150 ms for hover and press colors, 200 ms for a change of state, 260 ms for a status line swapping in place (`motion.swap`), and 300 ms for a scrim. Something leaving, such as a dialog closing, leaves on `out` in 200 ms, so a closed dialog never lingers.
- **Press:** a button goes down to 0.955 in 70 ms when pressed, a round icon button to 0.88 and a list row to 0.975, and springs back on `pop` when let go. Hover changes only the fill.
- **Dialogs and sheets:** on a phone a dialog that takes the screen rises from the bottom on `slide` and sinks back; from the tablet layout up it is a panel that rises a little and fades in on `soft`. A short dialog, such as a confirmation, and a menu pop in from slightly smaller on `pop`; on Android a menu does too, from its button's corner, over a scrim that fades. It lines up with its button's end edge, or with its start edge when the caller asks, as the composer's + does, and from a button in the lower half of the window it opens upward. It is placed by where its button is on screen, so beside a conversation, on a tablet or an unfolded phone, it stays by that button. Where the room past the button is short, as in a narrow roster, it narrows rather than run off the screen. On Android a pushed screen slides in over the one under it, which drifts back, and a form, Settings or a routine's runs rises from the bottom like a sheet; the system draws both.
- Avatars move continuously while they are on screen, on the budget in [Avatars](#avatars): only on-screen avatars step, list-size ones at half rate, and at most 24 at once. Their face eases into each pose; on the ground the body rides `pop`, as do the antenna's tip and the light, its lean rides `soft` and its gaze `slide`, so a bot settles as quickly as the chrome does; off the ground it falls. What a bot does is an act that replaces the one under way ([Life](#avatars)). A signal fades in over the state duration.
- **Panes and lines that open:** the details pane slides in on `slide` and out on `out` in 200 ms. It moves by a transform, never by its width, so the conversation beside it is laid out once, not on every frame, and the spring's overshoot never widens it. On the web the connection line unfolds above the app on `slide` and folds away the same way, keeping its last sentence while it goes; on Android it appears and goes in place.
- **Toasts** drop in from the top on `pop` and leave upward on `out` in 200 ms.
- **Loading** never jumps. What is still being read holds its place with skeleton rows the size of the rows they stand for, which appear only after 150 ms, so a quick read shows none, and then breathe slowly. Something that arrives in place of a skeleton or an empty space, such as an empty state's mark, pops in from slightly smaller on `pop`. Waiting with nothing to hold a place, such as connecting, shows three dots that pulse in turn. Connecting offers signing out, and in the desktop app another server, only once the wait passes eight seconds, so a load that is merely slow does not invite either; their place is kept from the start, so the dots above do not move when they arrive.
- **Entering the roster:** a bot or a room that was not listed before, whoever made it, arrives as one moment, staged as the first run stages its cast and keyed to the bot's landing. A light in the bot's own color comes up in its row over 320 ms on `out` (a warm white in the dark appearance, where the bot's color would glow too much): a cone from above the row, soft at its edge, and a pool at the row's floor. 180 ms later the avatar sets off and falls into it ([an entrance](#avatars)); the row clips it to its own height, so it comes in through the row's top edge and never crosses the row above. As it lands the pool takes the landing, a shadow settles under it, its antenna lights, and its name and then its line come in, 90 ms apart, on `soft`, from just below. The light holds for 520 ms more and goes down over 640 ms on `out` (`stage.rowHold`, `stage.rowDim`). Nothing of it waits on a timer that can run ahead of the bot: the light goes only once the avatar has landed, and one no frame draws, as one scrolled out of view, counts as landed 900 ms after it set off (`stage.rowLand`). A room's faces come down one after the other, 140 ms apart, and its light follows the last. The row takes its full height from its first frame, and the rows under it make room on `slide`. Nothing of this plays for the rows a roster loads with, for what a search lists, or when more than three are new at once, as after a while away: those simply appear (`rosterArrivals`). Android plays the same moment from the same tokens, with the light drawn as feathered cones in SVG; only the web's rim of light on the avatar is the web's own.
- **Organizing the roster:** a bot the operator pins, moves behind the scenes or deletes sinks 6 px, shrinks to 0.94 and fades out of the rows on `out` in 220 ms, drawn where it stood, as the rows under it close up on `slide`, and so does one moved into a folded section; one moved into an open section or out of one travels there as a reordered row does. One pinned pops into the pinned band on `pop` from 0.6, the band growing a line when it needs one, and one unpinned shrinks away from the band as the others make room on `slide` and its row arrives among the rows. Folding a section fades its rows out as the rows under it close up, and opening it fades them in as the rows under it make room, while its chevron turns a quarter on `pop`. A room moved behind the scenes from its menu leaves the rows as such a bot does. A bot or room brought back to the list from behind the scenes fades away there as the rest close up. A row that only stops being listed, as after a search, goes at once, and nothing plays when the band first appears.
- **Roster reorder:** when the roster's order changes, usually because a bot spoke and rose to the top, rows travel to their new places instead of jumping. The order is the roster's own; the motion only draws the change ([`rosterMotion.ts`](../../packages/client-runtime/src/presentation/rosterMotion.ts)).
  - The rows that left the others' order (the shortest set whose removal leaves the rest in order; in a swap, the one that rose) lift: to 1.02 over an opaque ground with the `lift` shadow, above the rows they pass, and the row the latest move lifted above one still up from a move before. They travel on `slide` and are set down as they arrive. A row that rose makes its bot hop once.
  - The rows they pass step aside on `slide` 40 ms later. A row that only came or went appears or leaves in place, a new row fading in on `soft` as the others make room, and the rows it pushes along step at once. Everything else stays put.
  - A burst, several bots speaking within a second, retargets every row from where it is drawn: a row already travelling goes on toward its new place at once, and a row already lifted stays up without hopping again. Changes that land in one render are one move.
  - Nothing moves on the first rows drawn or when the operator's own search changes what is listed: results just appear.
  - The selected row stays selected, and nothing is announced: the rows move without a live region. On the web focus stays on the same row; on Android that has not been checked with TalkBack yet. Scrolled down, the first row in view that does not itself lift keeps its place, so rows moving above the view never push what the operator is looking at; at the top, the list stays at the top, where a row that rises comes into view.
  - **On the web** the list measures each row just before React moves it and plays the difference back (FLIP) as a `translate` each row's own spring writes on the frame loop, every row whose place on screen changed, the ones the list's own scroll moved included. A row already travelling is rebased onto its new place without touching its speed, so a burst bends its way and never stops it. The lift is CSS `scale` and an opacity, which nothing else writes. **On Android** every entry of the list has a height known before it is drawn, worked out from how tall a line of `text-base`, `text-sm` and `text-xs` is at the operator's font size, which the app reads once from a line of each drawn out of sight and again when the font size changes, and from how many lines, one or two, each bot's title takes at the list's width, which the app measures out of sight as titles and widths change (at the default size, a row 64 px with no title, and a line of title more for each of its lines, a section's header 48 px and its empty line 40 px, the behind-the-scenes row 56 px, a pinned bot 94 px and the band by its lines), so the list knows each one's place without measuring it and how far the row it keeps in place ([`keptRow`](../../packages/client-runtime/src/presentation/rosterMotion.ts)) moved, in px. A row that keeps its order but starts somewhere else, as under a band that grew, steps to its new place too. Each moved row's list cell carries its own Reanimated layout transition, which runs on the UI thread. The list has no scroll of its own to keep its place with. After the rows it holds a mark, nothing to see, and the scroll view keeps the mark where it is on screen (`maintainVisibleContentPosition`): as it mounts a frame that moved the mark down the list, it scrolls that far, before the frame is drawn. The mark moves as far as the kept row, in a layout transition on the same `slide` with the same wait, started with the rows' own, and Reanimated mounts every transition's frame in one go, so the kept row's step and the scroll that answers it reach the screen in the same frame, and the row holds still but for the rounding of each to a whole pixel. A burst retargets the mark with the rows; where it changes which row is kept while the mark is still travelling, the newly kept row first drifts by what was left of that travel. The operator dragging the list does not stop any of it: the scroll view adds what the mark moved to the finger's own scroll, so the kept row stays with the finger. That is read in the source of React Native 0.88.1 and Reanimated 4.7.1, and has not been recorded on a device. The list keeps every row attached while off screen, so a row crossing the view from above it to below it is drawn on its way. Under reduced motion the rows take their places at once and the mark with them, in one frame, so a scrolled list still keeps its place.
- **In a conversation:**
  - Opening one, its entries fade up a few pixels into place, on `soft`.
  - The operator's message leaves the composer at once: it rises from the composer, shrunk, and pops into its place at the end on `pop`, while the send button's arrow lifts away on `out` and the composer settles back on `pop`.
  - A bot's message settles in on `soft`, at its full size from the start so nothing below it moves, and its text shows at a reading pace on `out`, longer for more (a third of a second to about a second).
  - Cards, notices and dates that arrive settle in the same way. What was there when the conversation opened appears in place, and on the web so do the chips of files a saved draft already held; only what arrives while it is on screen moves. A phone's chips appear in the text without moving.
  - At its end, the conversation stays pinned to the newest entry as entries arrive. Scrolled up, nothing moves it: messages others write are counted in a "New messages" pill over the end (cards, notices and times are not messages), and well away from the end a round button goes back to it. While the bot waits on a card less than half of which shows, wherever the operator is, the pill reads "Waiting for you" in `waiting`, with an arrow up or down toward the card, and brings it to the middle of the view; it takes the place of the other two, and while it shows, the conversation keeps its last entry clear of it. All of them pop in and out on `pop`. Sending a message returns to the end.
  - A card that closes while on screen marks it: its actions fold away on `slide`, the outcome settles in on `soft`, an allowed approval draws its check and pulses once on `pop`, and a chosen option's check pops in on `pop`.
  - A turn that starts brings a small copy of its bot, 24 px and in the turn's state, into the timeline where the reply will appear, on `pop`. Beside it, one status line saying, in plain words, what it is doing: "Running a command", "Reading a file", "Searching the web". It never counts the time: how long the turn took is the fold's to say once it is over. When the line changes, the old one leaves upward as the new one rises in, over 260 ms on `out` (`motion.swap`); the conversation header and the roster row change their line the same way, from the same state.
  - A turn that ends folds its row in place into one quiet line, which pops in on `pop`, as tall as the row was; a reply the turn wrote comes in below it, so nothing above moves. A turn that has done no work keeps its row at the end, below anything the bot says meanwhile, until it ends: then the row goes and leaves the reply, or, for a turn stopped or failed before its first step, becomes a line saying so ("Stopped"), with no steps to open.
  - A work fold's chevron turns on `pop` and its steps open and close on `slide`.
  - Something running carries a small live mark, a dot that breathes. It is stepped rather than drawn every frame, so a screen of busy bots does not hold the display at full refresh rate: it changes 8 times in 1.4 s.
  - **A turn's plan** is a short card under the turn's row, indented to its words: each step one line 26 px tall (28 px on Android), a ring for a step still to do, a check on `muted` for one done, the step being done in `foreground` at medium weight. The card settles in on `soft`. A step that ends in front of the operator draws its check and pops on `pop`. One **live mark**, a ring in the bot's color with the live dot inside, stands on the step being done: it pops in on `pop` where the first step runs, and goes from the step that ended to the one that starts on `slide`, drawn from the spring itself on the web's frame loop and by a Reanimated spring on Android, so a plan that moves on before the mark arrived bends its way. A step whose words change swaps them as a status line does. A plan too long for six lines shows the step before the first one not done, that step and what follows, with a quiet line that counts the steps before and one for those after. When the turn ends the card gives way to one line, "Plan done, 4 of 4", which pops in on `pop` and opens every step on `slide`; on the web the row's height closes on `slide` meanwhile, so what follows the turn does not jump.
  - **A bot's little ones** ([Little ones](#little-ones)) move from one model, which both surfaces step: on the web as one more member of the page's frame loop, on Android in a frame callback on the UI thread, on the avatars' own terms (not off screen, not on a screen behind another, not while the list scrolls). Each stage of a little one's life is solved, not stepped, so a frame of any length draws the same motion. Its numbers are `litter` in [`springs.ts`](../../packages/design-tokens/src/springs.ts).
    - **The split.** The block under the bot's face grows by a line for each one on `slide`, which is what moves the plan down to make room. The bot braces: it squashes deep, looks down, and rocks side to side three times, for 0.46 s before the first of several, 0.16 s before one alone. Then they come out from under it 170 ms apart, each from three tenths of its size, growing on `pop`, thrown down at 95 px a second and falling at 760 px a second squared, held to the bot by a strand of the bot's color until it is 27 px away. The bot springs up off each one in a small hop, and its antenna whips. The first out goes furthest down, so none passes another. A little one lands with a squash as deep as it came down fast, rocks once, looks up at the bot, and its words come in from 6 px aside on `soft`.
    - **The work.** At its line it bobs on its own beat and blinks. Only this, so a family at rest steps every other frame, as a small avatar does; while anything of it travels it steps on every frame.
    - **The return.** Its words leave on `out` in 160 ms. It ducks for 120 ms, then jumps up into the bot, still rising at 150 px a second as it arrives, shrinking to four tenths of its size over its last 110 ms, and drawn behind the ones that stand, which look up after it for 0.7 s. The bot stretches up to meet it, then gulps: its eye shuts, it squashes, swells by a tenth on `pop` and settles, and its light glows once. The lines below close up on `slide` from the moment it jumps.
    - **A failure.** It loses its color, over 200 ms on `out` on the web and at once on Android, slumps, and keeps its line for 0.95 s. Then it ducks for 220 ms and goes home in a jump that only just reaches the bot, which takes it in with a blink, a small swell and a look down, and no glow.
    - **Never restarting.** One told it ended while it is still falling lands first; one that had not come out yet never does; one that joins while others are coming out follows them without a second brace. The bot's own answers are acts of its avatar ([Life](#avatars)), so each replaces what the bot was doing and four in a row rise no higher than one hop.
    - Where the family does not move, off screen, over the cap of 24, or on a surface that is not in front, each one is simply at its line and one that ended is simply gone. A family there when a conversation opens is simply there. When the turn ends its family goes with the bot's face, and the block closes on `slide`.
  - **In the roster** a split bot's dots pop in and out on `pop`, one for each little one at work.
- **First run:** a short the operator plays, on one stage that stays up from the welcome to the arrival while only its set changes. Its times and distances are the `stage` values beside the springs in [`springs.ts`](../../packages/design-tokens/src/springs.ts), and which set a step has is `stageScene` in the client runtime, so both platforms change the same things at the same steps.
  - **The stage.** On the web it is dark: it takes the dark appearance whatever the app's, with a haze of the bot's color from above, the dark closing in at the edges, and a floor lit in that color. On Android it keeps the app's appearance and its lights are of each bot's color, so they show on a light canvas too.
  - **A light** is drawn the way stage light is seen ([decision 0065](../decisions/0065-the-first-run-tells-what-j3-bots-is-before-it-asks.md)). A lamp hangs above the stage, every lamp at the same height, 332 px above its bot's feet. Its **cone** is brightest by the lamp and thins toward the floor; across it, it is fully lit over a little more than the bot's width and falls away to nothing over as much again, as an angle about the lamp, so the soft edge widens as the cone does. On the web the lamp is warm white with three parts in ten of its bot's color. A little **dust** hangs in the lead's cone and rises slowly. Where the cone lands is an oval **pool**, hot at its middle, and the floor is **dark where the bot stands**. On the web the lit bot has a thin bright **rim** along its top and some of its color in the air around it; a bot with no light of its own is drawn dark, at four tenths of its brightness, never see-through, with an edge of the color that spills on it, on the side the lit bot is. The web draws all of it with gradients, masks and filters, as layers the compositor fades; Android draws the cone as seven cones one inside another, the pool and the shadow with `react-native-svg`, and shows a bot without a light at a third of its strength. How a bot is lit in a scene, in its own light at full strength or softer, in what spills from another's, or under the light everyone shares, is `stageLight` in the client runtime.
  - **The cast comes down.** Five bots, the lead at the front in the middle and four around and behind it, each a little higher the further upstage, the lead in its light at full strength and the others in theirs a little softer. Each has a light, which comes on over 320 ms on `out`, 180 ms before its bot sets off; the bots follow one another 140 ms apart, each with [an entrance](#avatars). The welcome's lines arrive as they do.
  - **The light takes turns.** While the cast says what it does, one bot at a time has the light, from one side of the stage to the other, 2.1 s each: it hops as the light comes to it and speaks, its line pulling into focus under the stage, and the others stand dark in what spills from it. The last turn lights everyone, who nod, and it rests there.
  - **One light for all.** While the cast thinks, each bot's light gives way to one wide cone from one lamp over the whole stage, cool where theirs are warm, breathing between three quarters and all of its strength every 3.6 s, with its pool across the floor. Every bot shows its thinking pose.
  - **Lines pull into focus.** A step's title, lines and controls arrive one after another, 90 ms apart, from 10 px of blur and 14 px below, clearing on `soft`; the step before fades and blurs away in 160 ms on `out`, so the new lines never wait for it. Steps do not slide: the camera says where the operator is. Android has no blur to pull: its lines rise in on `soft` as they fade in.
  - **The camera** closes in on the lead, to 1.18, when the steps start to be about it, pulls back to wide for the welcome and to 0.94 for the finale. On the web it is the stage's scale, about the middle of the floor, on `soft` from the frame loop, so a step taken before the last move settled bends into the next. The rest of the cast steps back into the dark, its lights off, drawn as a bot with no light is, and watches. On Android the lead is carried on `slide` from the cast to its place above the steps, grown to 1.18, and back.
  - **The lead answers.** Each step it nods. It wears the name under its feet, in a tag that pops in on `pop`, and at each key typed it looks down at the field and bobs. A new shape is taken in a squash and a small pop; a new color is a hop, and on the web the stage's light crosses over to the new color in 300 ms. While the step that connects a provider has the operator's eye, its light closes to 0.66 of its width.
  - **The finale** is one move of the light. Once the bot exists its light swells to one and a half times its width in a quarter of a second and settles a little wider than it was, 0.9 s in all on `out`, while a glow of its color opens around the bot and goes, soft at every edge and drawn under the step's words. The camera pulls back, the cast's lights come up and each bot cheers, one after another across the stage, 110 ms apart. Nothing else on the stage is scaled meanwhile: the bot keeps its size. Its name pops in 260 ms after the light starts and the line under it rises 160 ms after that. The arrival stays 1.5 s, by when all of it has played. On Android the cast has left with the story, so the lead cheers alone in its swelling light.
  - Its conversation then opens without sliding, and the bot travels from the stage into the conversation's header on `slide`, where its avatar is. The web draws that with a view transition, and a browser without them opens the conversation at once. Android draws the bot in flight over the screens and hides the header's avatar until it lands.
  - **Never waiting.** Every control is live from its first frame, so Continue pressed mid-entrance cuts to the next set from wherever the stage is. Nothing here holds a step the flow needs: the forms work as their lines arrive.
  - The bots' motion is the avatars', on the frame loop or the UI thread; the lights, the dust and the lines are opacities, transforms and a short blur, which the compositor plays; a bot's rim and its darkening are a filter on the bot, drawn again only while the bot itself moves.
- **Reduced motion** stops every move and every loop: nothing scales, slides or springs, entries appear in place, a status line changes in place, the live mark, the skeletons and the avatars stand still, colors still change, and each state keeps its still form. A turn under way keeps its still avatar and its line. The first run keeps only crossfades: a step fades over 200 ms, the cast is simply there with its lights on, the dust stands still, the light everyone shares does not breathe, the camera is where it is going at once, what the bots do is one list under the whole cast lit instead of a line at a time, the finale's light is simply up, and the conversation opens without the bot's travel. A bot new to the roster fades in with its row over 200 ms, with no light and no fall, and one that leaves is gone. A bot's little ones appear by their lines and leave with no travel, the bot does not squash or swell for them, one that failed stays grey at its line for a moment and is gone, and a plan's live mark is on its step at once.

## Components

### Primitives

Every surface is built from the same few parts, each owning its look in the `ui` layer (`apps/web/src/components/ui`, `apps/mobile/src/ui.tsx`); callers choose a variant.

| Part | Look |
| --- | --- |
| Button | A pill, semibold. Variants: secondary, the default for every action that is not the one a surface exists for (filled `muted`, darkening to `muted-strong` on hover); primary, for that one (filled with `accent`, the text color); outline, only where an action needs a boundary, as among filled fields; ghost, a quieter ghost, and destructive filled or outlined in `blocked`. Icon buttons are round and take the deeper press |
| Field | Filled with `muted`, darker on hover, with no outline until it has focus, when it takes the focus ring; a field the operator got wrong takes a `blocked` border. Read-only text is muted |
| Choice row | A rounded row with a box or a circle, `input` outlined, taking the row's press |
| Switch | A pill, `control-on` when on and `control-off` when off, whose thumb, lifted by the `thumb` shadow, springs across on `pop` |
| Checkbox | A 16 px box with the 6 px radius, filled with the text color when checked |
| Menu | A `surface` popover with `overlay` elevation, the 16 px radius, and rows of the 12 px radius that fill with `muted` when highlighted. A row may lead with an icon and carry a quieter second line, as New's "New room" does when a room cannot be made yet. A row that cannot be undone is drawn in `blocked`. A row that leads to more rows, as a bot's More, ends in a chevron and keeps the menu open: the new rows fade in in place of the others, with a Back row first. A choice already made, as the section a bot is in, ends in a check. On Android it is the app's own menu, not the system's alert, so it looks and reads the same; its width is set, so at a large font size a row's text wraps onto a second line inside it |
| Segmented control | Two to four short choices side by side in one `muted` pill; the picked one is a raised `surface` segment. A radio group to assistive technology. Longer or more choices, such as languages, stay a list |
| Skeleton | Rows of `muted` blocks in the shape of what is loading, a mark and two lines each, the same on both platforms and wherever something loads: a bot's routines and memory, a memory file, a routine's runs, usage and settings. Read to screen readers as "Loading…" |
| Dialog | A `surface` panel with the 20 px radius and `overlay` elevation over the `scrim`; on a phone, the whole screen. Its title is in the display face. On Android a confirmation is a short panel of its own over the scrim, its answer that keeps things first; a name it asks for, as a section's, is a field in the same panel that takes the keyboard as it opens, its answer that acts waiting for a name that is new and not empty |
| Roster row | Rounded, tinted toward `muted` on hover and filled with it when selected, taking the row's press |
| Profile | The head of a bot's or a room's details: the picture at 96 px, centered: a bot's live avatar, which moves with its state as in the roster, or a room's two members, still, the name in the display face with its badge ("Primary", "Archived"), quiet lines under it (a bot's job, a room's member count) and the edit button. The details of a bot or room behind the scenes say so in one quiet line after Stop, with Bring back to the list, which puts it back in the list and leaves the details open. Below it the sections are set apart by space, not rules, and a destructive action stands alone below a hairline |

### Visuals

A visual is shown, not attached. It is a card above the text of the message it belongs to, where the operator meets the picture before the sentence that explains it, at a card's width so a chart has room, in the card frame: the bubble's radius, a hairline border, `surface`. The page fills the card, its own padding 12 px. Under it a caption on a hairline: the visual's title, one line cut short, and an expand button, "Open {title} full screen", that opens it in a dialog titled with it, the whole screen on a phone and a wide sheet on a larger one. Escape or Close returns to the button.

The page takes the height it reports, up to 2000 px, so a short table is short and nothing scrolls inside a conversation that already scrolls; a taller page scrolls inside its card. While it loads, the card holds the height the bot gave, with a quiet placeholder in it. A page that does not show in ten seconds, or whose markup embeds another page or a declarative shadow root, is replaced by one muted line, "Couldn't show this visual.", and the card keeps its title, so the operator knows what is missing and the message still reads.

A visual matches the app because it is drawn with the app's colors: the page's `:root` carries the interface tokens of the current theme as CSS variables (`--background`, `--foreground`, `--muted-foreground`, `--border`, `--accent`, `--success`, `--chart-1` to `--chart-6` and the rest, with `--font-sans`, `--font-mono` and `--radius`), and the page is drawn again when the theme changes. The `say` tool's description lists them for the bot.

## Words

Interface text follows the same rules as bot messages: plain, short, literal.

- Sentence case everywhere.
- A button says what it does to what: "Pause routine", not "Pause".
- Schedules are written out: "Weekdays at 8:47 AM", not a cron expression. An interval reads as one ("Every 15 minutes", "Every 6 hours at minute 00") only when it divides the hour or the day evenly; `*/7` minutes runs at :56 and again at :00, so it is not "every 7 minutes". A schedule with no plain sentence shows its expression. A schedule kept in another time zone names it, hourly ones included: "Every Monday and Thursday at 9:00 AM (Western European Time)", "Every hour at minute 15 (India Standard Time)". A single run gives its full date and time in the operator's time zone, and "Next run" is always in the operator's time zone.
- Times use the language's own short style ("8:47 AM" in English, "08:47" in Portuguese) and the operator's time zone. A time of day never breaks across lines: the space inside "8:47 AM" is a no-break space wherever a time appears, a full-width one, since the narrow one some runtimes use is set too thin at small sizes to read as a gap, in rosters, schedules, last runs and notices. A time standing alone reads "Yesterday at 1:40 PM"; inside a sentence it has its own form ("yesterday at 1:40 PM", "at 8:47 AM", "on Nov 13, 2026, 4:30 PM"), so no capital lands mid-sentence.
- Errors say what happened and what the operator can do.
- No exclamation marks, no mascot voice.

### Languages

Every interface sentence goes through the translation layer as a whole sentence with named placeholders. English is the source; Brazilian Portuguese is the first translation. A catalog that misses a key or drops a placeholder does not type-check. Plurals follow each language's rules. The language follows the browser unless the operator picks one in Settings, where each language is named in itself.

## Accessibility

- Text contrast of at least 4.5:1, and 3:1 for large text and interface marks, in both themes.
- Disabled controls are exempt from those minimums, as WCAG 1.4.3 exempts inactive components: a disabled control is drawn at half opacity, which is what marks it disabled, and it keeps its name for a screen reader.
- State is never carried by color alone: the avatar's form or a mark changes too.
- Every action is reachable by keyboard on desktop and by screen reader on every platform, with the same name it has on screen.
- Motion: everything honors the platform's reduced-motion setting (see [Motion](#motion)).
- Touch targets are at least 44 px. On touch screens a compact control grows to a real 44 px box, so controls that sit side by side or wrap onto a second line never cover one another; pointer devices keep the compact size. Menu rows, such as New, do the same.
- Light and dark follow the system unless the operator picks one in Settings, including a change the system made while the app was in the background. The choice is applied before the first paint: on the desktop the window opens with that theme's canvas as its background, and on Android 12 and later the splash takes it too. What the system draws for the app, its notifications and the desktop's tray, stays in the system's theme.
