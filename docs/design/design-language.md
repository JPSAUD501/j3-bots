# Design language

**Status:** the chrome is the Pips direction ([decision 0018](../decisions/0018-the-interface-follows-pips.md)) on the web, the desktop app, which draws the web build, and Android, and so are the [avatars](#avatars) and the [bot palette](#bot-palette). The conversation and the first run are being rebuilt for it, each by its own change; until then they keep their earlier form. The values below are the ones in [`packages/design-tokens`](../../packages/design-tokens/src/tokens.ts), and the contrast figures are checked by its tests. The web client's development build has a gallery at `/gallery` that shows every component in every state, in both themes, with the springs to replay; it is the place to review the design, and it is not part of a production build. Nothing of it has run on iOS or iPadOS yet.

## Two references, one product

J3 Bots' interface is a deliberate mix of two existing designs. Both are described here in words; neither's assets are reproduced.

**From persistent-agent products (xAI's Grok Bot is the clearest example):** the interface of a messaging app, organized around who you are talking to.

- The sidebar is a roster of named teammates, not a history of chats. Each row is an avatar, a name, a time, and one line that reads like a status update from a person.
- The avatar is the presence indicator. It changes with what the bot is doing, so there is no separate spinner or status badge. The one mark an avatar can carry is the primary bot's, which names a role, not a state.
- Conversations are bubbles. The operator's are dark and right-aligned, the bot's are light and left-aligned, grouped, with timestamps between groups.
- The chrome is almost monochrome. Color belongs to the bots.
- Structured answers arrive as cards in the same timeline as prose.
- A side pane shows what the bot is and what runs without you: its routines.
- Controls that do not help the operator delegate are removed.

**From T3 Code:** the precision and density of a developer tool.

- A true-black dark canvas and near-white light canvas, zinc neutrals, hairline borders, small radii.
- A compact type scale where a mouse leads: 15 px messages, 12 px metadata.
- A finished turn's work folds into one line ("Worked for 2m") that opens to show each step.
- A command palette that reaches every action, and a shortcut for everything.
- Semantic color tokens as CSS variables with a light and a dark value each.
- Components in a `ui` layer own their look; callers choose a variant.

**The synthesis.** J3 Bots looks like a messaging app and behaves like a professional tool. Messaging gives the structure: roster, bubbles, presence. The developer tool gives the manner: density, keyboard reach, and work that is always inspectable but never in the way.

**Pips.** The bots are where the life is: small characters that look at the operator and show what they are doing with their bodies, on chrome that stays quiet, near-monochrome and precise. Motion is quick and springy, and it serves the bots and the operator's hands, never decoration ([decision 0018](../decisions/0018-the-interface-follows-pips.md)).

## Principles

1. **Who before what.** Every screen starts from a bot. There is no screen that lists tasks, runs or sessions as first-class things.
2. **One line of truth.** Each roster row carries one line: what the bot last said, or what it is doing now, or what it needs. The row that needs the operator is visibly different from the rest.
3. **Work is available, not present.** The steps of a turn are one tap away and never open by themselves.
4. **Color means someone or something.** A color on screen is a bot's identity or a state (waiting, blocked, error). Decoration does not use color.
5. **Lively bots, quiet chrome.** The bots move; the chrome around them answers the operator's hand and then keeps still. Nothing moves that reduced motion would not stop.
6. **Same words everywhere.** A bot, a room, a routine, a card: the same nouns on every surface and in every prompt.

## Layout

Three regions, the same on every surface.

| Region | Wide (1200 px and up) | Tablet (768 to 1199 px) | Phone (below 768 px) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Roster | Left column, 280 px | Left column, 280 px | First screen |
| Conversation | Center, text column capped at 720 px | Center | Pushed screen |
| Details | Right pane, 320 px, closed by default, beside the conversation | Modal dialog over the conversation, with a scrim that closes it | Modal dialog that covers the screen |

- The layout follows the width of the interface's own container, not the window's, so the same component lays out correctly in a narrow frame on a wide screen.
- The conversation text column has a maximum width; on a wide window the space goes to margins and the details pane, not to longer lines.
- The details pane opens from the bot's name in the conversation header, or from the pane button beside it. On a phone the name carries a chevron, because there is no pane button. At the wide layout the conversation gives up the pane's width at once and the pane slides in from the end edge into it; closing, the pane slides out and the conversation takes the width back once it has gone. As a dialog it slides in from the end edge over its scrim, the left edge in a right-to-left language. Either way the details keep their own width while they move, so nothing in them reflows.
- Below the wide layout the details pane is a modal dialog: the roster and the conversation behind it are inert, focus is trapped inside it, the dialog handles `Escape`, and the conversation's shortcuts wait until it closes. At the wide layout it sits beside the conversation, nothing else is blocked, and `Escape` closes it from anywhere in the shell.
- On phones every region keeps clear of the screen's safe areas (the notch, rounded corners and home indicator) on the edges it touches, in portrait and landscape.

### Keyboard and focus

| Action | Keys |
| --- | --- |
| Next and previous bot or room, in the order the roster draws them (pinned bots, the list, then each section; hidden bots and those in a folded section are skipped); with none open, the first or the last | `Alt+↓`, `Alt+↑` (`⌥↓`, `⌥↑` on macOS) |
| Put the cursor in the composer | `/` |
| Stop the bot that is working, or whose background work holds a change of its settings | `Ctrl+.` (`⌘.` on macOS) |
| Close the details pane, before anything else | `Escape` |
| Move through the roster | `↓` from the search field, then `↑`, `↓`, `Home`, `End` |

- Shortcut hints are shown where the action is: on the Stop row in the bot's details, and on the empty screen for the next-bot shortcut. Touch screens show neither. Only a coarse pointer hides anything: with no pointer at all, as with a keyboard alone or a TV remote, every hint and the inline "Waiting for you" stay.
- The roster's arrow keys are unmodified ones; `Alt+↑` and `Alt+↓` pass through it to the shell.
- Opening the details pane moves focus to its heading; closing it on the same conversation, by any route, returns focus to the conversation title. Choosing another conversation with a mouse or keyboard puts the cursor in its composer; on touch screens it does not, so the keyboard does not cover the conversation. Focus never moves under a dialog: one left open over a conversation a notification opened keeps it. When the details close because another conversation was chosen, the move wins and focus follows it. Loading a page moves focus nowhere.
- Each conversation opens at its own newest entry, never where the previous one was scrolled to.
- A command palette (`Cmd/Ctrl+K`) that reaches every action is planned and not built yet.

## Color

Near-monochrome neutrals carry the interface, on a true-black dark canvas. The focus ring, links, unread and the primary action are the text color, so the chrome adds no hue of its own. Semantic colors mark state. A separate palette belongs to bots.

Elevation is for what lies over other content. `raised` is a faint shadow under a card in light and none in dark; `overlay`, under popovers, menus, dialogs and sheets, is a soft drop shadow in light and, since no shadow shows on black, a darker one with a hairline of white at 7% in dark; `thumb` lifts a switch's thumb off its track; `lift` is under a roster row while it travels to a new place, a drop shadow in light and, in dark, a deeper one of black at 90% with a hairline of white at 9%, written in `rgba()` so Android's `boxShadow` draws the same. Tailwind's own shadow scale is removed, so only these exist, and a web test fails on a shadow class outside them.

Destructive actions use `blocked`: there is no separate destructive red. Text is never drawn with a text token at reduced opacity, since that would put it below the measured contrast; the one exception is a disabled control (see [Accessibility](#accessibility)).

### Bot palette

Each bot has one color, chosen by the operator from twelve: tomato, tangerine, honey, lime, mint, teal, sky, cobalt, iris, orchid, rose and slate. They run round the hue circle, and slate is the one quiet color. Each has a light and a dark value, kept apart from the interface tokens in `packages/design-tokens/src/botPalette.ts`. The operator, or a bot asked to change its avatar, can choose only these names, and the server refuses any other; a client draws slate for a color it does not know.

The light values sit darker than the dark ones so that a body reads as a shape on every canvas. What the palette guarantees, and what the tests check:

- every body reaches 3:1 against `background`, `surface`, `sidebar` and `muted` in its theme (measured minimum 3.19 in light, mint on `muted`, and 3.86 in dark, cobalt on `muted`), and against `avatar-ink`, white in light and near-black in dark, which the interface draws over a bot's color (minimum 3.63, mint, and 4.33, cobalt);
- every pair of bodies stays at least 0.085 apart in OKLab for typical vision (measured minimum 0.092 in both themes, honey and lime in light, teal and sky in dark).

Twelve saturated hues cannot all stay apart under a color vision deficiency: simulated after Machado, Oliveira and Fernandes (2009) at full severity, protanopia and deuteranopia bring the warm hues together, and cobalt and iris, to within 0.02. The silhouette carries the difference instead: the cast gives the first twelve bots twelve different shapes, and color is never the only thing that tells two bots apart.

The eye is the same in both themes: a white lens and a near-black pupil (`#141418`), 18.4:1 apart. On the palest bodies in dark (honey, lime) the lens stands only 1.7:1 from the body, so the eye reads by its pupil. An offline bot's body keeps 12% of its saturation, the web's `saturate(0.12)`, and is drawn at half opacity.

A bot's bubbles take a tint of its color: its palette value mixed over the canvas in OKLab, 11% in light and 28% in dark, where a lighter tint would vanish, with its chroma reduced where the mix leaves sRGB, as light tomato's does (`botBubble`, beside the palette in `botPalette.ts`, emitted as `bot-bubble-{color}`); the tests hold the text and code in them to 4.5:1 for every color in both themes. The composer's focus border and its ready send button take the bot's color itself, which reaches 3:1 on `surface` like every fill of the palette (3.63 in light and 4.18 in dark at the lowest), and the send arrow on it is `avatar-ink`. Each client reads a bot's tint and color through one helper (`botTint`), so where a bot's color comes from can change in one place.

## Avatars

The avatar is the most important element on screen: it is how the operator recognizes a bot at a glance and how they read its state.

Requirements:

- Recognizable at 24 px in a list and expressive at 96 px in a profile.
- Built from two choices the operator makes, a **shape** and a **color**, so that a roster of twelve bots has twelve distinct silhouettes.
- Carries every state through its own form, with motion only as an addition.
- Works as a still image for notifications and for reduced-motion settings.

An avatar is a **Pip**: a small character whose body is the bot's silhouette in its color, with one lens eye and an antenna. The body is the bot's identity; the eye and the antenna carry its state. The antenna's tip is the status light, so no badge and no spinner carry state. The one corner mark is the [primary bot's](#primary-mark), which says who the bot is, never what it is doing. The design is J3 Bots' own: no other product's characters, art or animation are reproduced.

**Shapes.** Twelve, drawn in a 100-unit box (`packages/design-tokens/src/avatarShapes.ts`) and listed in this order: dome, block, bean, loaf, drop, ears, horns, ghost, hex, gem, toes and pear. Each is an outline in absolute path commands only, so the PNG renderer draws exactly what the SVG renderers do, stroked in its own color to round its corners (pointed shapes take a thicker stroke). Every body stands on the same line, its eye and antenna root where it is widest and highest, and a renderer frames the same square for all of them (`avatarViewBox`), so bots of different shapes line up in a row.

**The cast.** The server gives a bot created without an avatar the pair of a shape and a color that the bots use least, and the pickers offer that same pair first (`freeAvatar` in `@j3-bots/design-tokens/names`). Of all 144 pairs it takes the one the fewest bots have as a pair, then the one whose shape and color the fewest bots have, then the first in order: the pairs of `avatarCast` first, then the rest by the cast's order of their shape and then of their color. The cast names each of the twelve shapes and twelve colors once, ordered so neighbors differ in hue, lightness and outline: a tangerine dome, a cobalt ears, a mint drop, an orchid horns, a honey ghost, an iris block, and so on. The first twelve bots therefore differ in both shape and color, no two bots share a pair until all 144 are taken, and a shape or color the operator chose for one bot is passed over for the next. The first run's welcome and the pairing screen float five of the first six, the cast's first in the middle, which is also the look the first bot starts with; the screens while the web client connects, and its wide layout with no conversation open, show the first three as the ensemble.

**States.** What an avatar shows comes from `avatarMood` in `packages/client-runtime`: what concerns the operator first, then a turn under way, then news.

| State | When | Eye | Antenna and light | Signal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Idle | Nothing running | Open, resting | Upright, the body's color | None |
| Thinking | A turn started, no visible work yet | Lid lowered, looking up and aside | Leans back | Three dots |
| Working | A turn is running tools | Narrowed on the work | Leans forward | A turning ring |
| Waiting | The bot asked a question or needs an approval | Wide, looking at the operator | Tall, light `waiting` | A ping from the light |
| Blocked | The turn cannot continue | Shut, tilted | Drooping, light `blocked` | None |
| Unread | Idle, with something the operator has not read | Wide, smiling, with cheeks | Light `foreground`, larger | None |
| Speaking | A message of the bot's is arriving | Open, lifted, the mouth moving | Upright | Sound arcs |
| Offline | This client has not seen the server for 3 s | Shut | Drooping, short | None; the body is washed out and drawn at half opacity |

- A bot that waits on the operator or is blocked shows it even while it speaks. A notification's still avatar shows what the notification means: waiting for a card, unread for a message or a handoff (`notificationState`).
- Speaking lasts from 0.9 to 2.8 s after the message's own time, longer for a longer message, so it plays while the conversation brings the words in and never again on a reload.
- A turn the server reports as possibly stalled holds its state's still form without its signal: a stuck bot stops moving and nothing at its light says the work goes on, so working and stuck never look the same, under reduced motion too.
- The light's three colors match the roster line's wording and color, and each state also changes the eye and the antenna, so state is never carried by color alone.
- A light in a state's color sits in a band of what the avatar is on, the way a room's front member is cut out of the one behind, so it stands clear of the body and the stalk whatever the bot's color, even where a blocked bot's antenna droops over its body (`lightCut`). A light in the body's color has no band: it is the antenna's tip, so an idle tomato or honey bot's tip never reads as a blocked or waiting light. The band takes the color of what the avatar is on at the moment, so it turns `muted` with a roster row or the conversation header while it is hovered, pressed or selected.
- Every state has a still form (`stillFace`): its pose held and its signal as one frame. It is what reduced motion shows, what notifications and the tray draw, and what small inline avatars (bubbles, the composer's mentions, cards, usage) use.
- Avatars of 28 px and under draw the eye, the stalk and the light larger so they stay legible.
- A room's avatar overlaps the bodies of its first two members, which sleep with every other bot while this client is offline.

**Life.** Each bot moves in its own way, from a seed hashed from its id (`avatarSeed`), the same on every device: how it leans its antenna, how fast it breathes, where its eye rests, when it blinks and glances. A change of state eases the face into the new pose and kicks the body once in the state's own way, on the interface's [springs](#motion), the hop and the antenna's lag on `pop` and the lean on `soft`: thinking tips its head, working ducks to it, waiting and unread hop, blocked slumps, and a bot back to idle blinks. Nothing plays when an avatar first appears. On a pointer device the eye follows the pointer in the states where the bot looks out at the operator. The picker's preview answers a change of shape, color or name with a small move of its own, and the first run's bot morphs into a new shape the same way.

**Rendering.** The model is pure and shared (`packages/client-runtime/src/presentation/avatar.ts`): a renderer steps it once a frame and draws the face through `avatarFrame` from the tokens.

- **Web:** one SVG per avatar and one frame loop for the page. A frame writes only the avatar's own SVG attributes, transforms for the body and the eye and the paths that bend (the antenna, the lids, the mouth), which repaint the avatar without laying out the page. It reads layout only after the pointer moved or the page scrolled, and before it writes, so drawing never forces a layout. An avatar out of view (IntersectionObserver) is not stepped, nor one asleep, which rests and would otherwise filter its washed-out body again each frame; one under 44 px steps every other frame, and at most 24 move at once, the largest first. The others hold their still form, and draw the new one when their state changes.
- **Android:** `react-native-svg`, with each avatar stepped on the UI thread by a Reanimated frame callback. The JS side decides whose callbacks run: none while the app is in the background, none off screen or on a screen out of focus, in the roster only the rows the list reports in view and none while it scrolls (each holds its pose until the list settles), under 44 px at half rate, and at most 24 at once, the largest first. A frame sets an SVG prop only when its value changed, and values are rounded to a quarter pixel, so a part that moved less than that is not drawn again. On the Pixel 8 emulator, with its software GPU and a release build, a roster of 20 bots drew 1% of its frames late at rest and 12% while scrolling, against 5% with every avatar still (**Verified**, before the body's springs moved to `pop` and `soft`; not measured since).
- **PNG:** the rasterizer in `packages/design-tokens/src/raster` draws the still forms for notifications and the tray.

**Image avatars** are not available yet: the contract's avatar is a shape and a color only.

### Primary mark

The primary bot ([Concepts](../product/concepts.md)) carries a mark at its avatar's lower end corner wherever its avatar names it: a roster row, a pinned bot and the conversation header, and on Android the details too. The details also keep a "Primary" badge beside the name. Small inline avatars, beside a message, in a mention or in a turn's row, go without it. The mark names a role, so it never changes with the bot's state, and the light still says what the bot is doing.

- A five-pointed star in `accent-foreground` on a disc of `accent`, the text color, so it reads as the chrome's own sign and takes no bot's color. The disc is 34% of the avatar's size and never under 14 px, so the star still reads on a 32 px header avatar.
- A band of what the avatar sits on surrounds the disc, 2 px wide, 3 px from a 56 px avatar up, cutting it out of the body the way a state's light is cut out ([`lightCut`](#avatars)). The band, not the disc's color, is what keeps it apart from the body, so it reads on all twelve colors in both themes, on a near-black slate in light and a near-white honey in dark alike. Like the light's band it takes the color of what the avatar is on at the moment, `muted` while a row is pressed or selected.
- Its center sits 16% of the avatar's size in from the corner, so it overlaps the body's edge. The antenna rises from the body's top and the eye sits high, so the corner never covers either.
- Screen readers hear it as "Primary bot" in the name of what holds the avatar, never as an image of its own.
- **On Android** it is drawn with the avatar (`PrimaryMark`, around `LiveBotAvatar`).

### App icons

Every platform's icon is one Pip: an iris loaf seen close on a true-black plate. The plate frames its eye, lifted and a little wide, and its antenna standing straight up with its light in the body's own color; the body rises off the plate's bottom edge and runs past its sides, so the black above it is the plate. The black plate carries it on a light wallpaper and a dark one alike. It is the avatar drawn by the avatar's own geometry (`avatarFrame`), at an icon's size with an avatar's small-size proportions, so the icon and the bots are one design. `packages/design-tokens/src/appIconArt.ts` holds it: the avatar, the pose, the plate's color, and the two framings it is drawn in, close on its plate, or whole where there is no plate. At 16 px, the smallest a platform shows it (a browser tab, the tray on macOS and Windows), the light at the antenna's tip is drawn half as large again: at its own size it is barely wider than the stalk and blurs into it, leaving a line over an eye, and grown it stands apart as a knob, so the antenna still says a Pip. `pnpm --filter @j3-bots/design-tokens generate` writes every native file from it to `packages/design-tokens/generated/icons`, and a test fails when they are stale:

- **Desktop:** the close framing on a rounded plate, from 16 to 1024 px for Linux and macOS, and in one icon file with each size Windows draws, from 16 to 256 px, each drawn at its size. macOS gets its own plate, inset as its icons are. The tray shows the Pip whole, without a plate, in the theme the system draws its tray in: at rest its eye open and its light the body's color. While anything needs the operator it takes the waiting state's still form: the eye wide, the antenna taller, and its light grown and ringed in the waiting color, so the change shows in the tip's size and shape and not only its color, since on Linux and Windows no count stands beside the tray.
- **Android:** an adaptive icon, the Pip over a background layer in the plate's black. The close framing sits in the part every mask (circle, squircle, rounded square) keeps, and the body carries on past it for a mask that shows more. The themed icon is the Pip whole as a silhouette, its lens cut out, which the launcher tints. The splash shows the Pip whole on each theme's canvas, in that theme's palette. A notification's icon is the Pip whole as a white silhouette, its lens cut out, on transparency, 96 px, since Android draws it from its alpha only and the opaque launcher icon would show as a white square; the notification tints it with the Pip's iris, which reads on a light shade and a dark one where the chrome's near-black accent would not.
- **iOS:** the close framing on a square plate, which iOS rounds, on no background for the dark icon, and as a silhouette for the tinted one.
- **Web:** the same icons, drawn as the web client builds. The favicon is an icon file with each size from 16 to 64 px, the close framing drawn at its size on its rounded plate, so it reads on a light tab strip and a dark one alike.

## Typography

Three faces, bundled with every client under the SIL Open Font License, so the app works offline and loads nothing from a font service ([the fonts and how they were cut](../../packages/design-tokens/fonts/README.md)):

| Face | Use | Weights |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Bricolage Grotesque** | Display: page and dialog titles, the gallery, a bot's name on its profile, the first run. Set tight, at −0.035 em | 700 |
| **Instrument Sans** | Everything that is read: messages, rows, controls, metadata | 400, 500, 600 |
| **JetBrains Mono** | Code, commands, identifiers and the steps of a turn, without ligatures, so `!=` and `->` read as typed | 400 |

- The files hold Latin, Latin-1, Latin Extended-A, general punctuation and arrows. A character outside them, as in a bot's name in another script, is drawn in the platform's font.
- On the web the faces are `@font-face` rules in the tokens' stylesheet, loaded from the bundle with the platform's font shown until they arrive. On Android they are embedded at build time, each family with its weights, so a weight picks its file; React Native has no inherited font for a `Text` on its own, so every `Text` and `TextInput` comes from the app's own `text.tsx`, which sets the face, and a lint rule refuses them from `react-native`. A `Text` nested in another sets no face unless it names one, so it inherits its parent's, as a line of a code block keeps the block's mono.
- **Weight:** text is regular (400) and medium (500); controls, a name that needs the operator, and buttons are semibold (600). Display is bold (700) only.
- Message text sets no line longer than about 70 characters.

### Type scale

The scale has five steps for text and four for headings, and each step has two sets of values: **touch**, for a phone read at arm's length, and **pointer**, for a screen where a mouse leads, read closer. Android always takes the touch set, in sp, so it follows the system's font size. The web takes the pointer set, in rem, and the touch set where its primary pointer is coarse (`@media (pointer: coarse)`), so the web on a phone reads as the Android app does. A screen names a step once (`text-sm`) and gets the right size on either; no screen sets a size of its own. Tailwind's own scale is removed, so only these steps exist.

| Step | Touch, size / line height | Pointer, size / line height | Used for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `2xs` | 13 / 18 | 12 / 16 | Times, statuses, the time between messages, the line that introduces a teammate's message |
| `xs` | 14 / 20 | 13 / 18 | Small labels, notices, code in a message; on the web, a roster row's line |
| `sm` | 16 / 22 | 14 / 20 | A roster row's line, hints; on the web, names, menu items and buttons |
| `base` | 17 / 22 | 15 / 22 | Names, menu items and a row's title; on the web, message text and the composer |
| `lg` | 17 / 24 | 16 / 24 | Message text and the composer on a touch screen |
| `xl` | 20 / 26 | 20 / 26 | Dialog and section titles |
| `2xl`, `3xl`, `4xl` | 24 / 28, 30 / 34, 40 / 42 | the same | The display face: a profile's name, a page's title, the first run |

**Why these sizes.** The scale was measured against the persistent-agent app this design takes its structure from, on one phone at one resolution, so pixels compared directly. Converted to Instrument Sans at the same x-height, that app sets message text, names, menu items, titles and the composer at about 17 sp, a list's second line at 16, a button at 14, and times and the lines between messages at 13. J3 Bots set them at 16, 14, 13 and 11. Both put about 24 sp between the lines of a message; the difference was the size of the letters, which made J3 Bots' names and metadata read small next to its messages. So:

- On touch, a name, a menu item and a message share one size, 17, and differ in leading: a paragraph needs 24 between its lines, a one-line name 22. Hierarchy comes from weight and color (a name is medium, a row's line is muted), not from shrinking the text under it.
- Nothing that is read is smaller than 13 on touch or 12 on a pointer screen. The 11 px step is gone: it was below what reads comfortably on a phone, and on a 1× desktop display.
- The pointer set keeps the developer tool's density: each step up to `base` is one pixel larger than before, with messages at 15 / 22, close to what chat apps on the desktop use, and the roster's names at 14.
- Instrument Sans has a tall x-height (0.51 em), so it reads about a size larger than a face of the same nominal size with a shorter one; the steps were chosen by x-height, not by nominal size.

**Font size settings.** Android's sizes are in sp, so the system's font size scales them, up to 200%. Line heights scale with them, so the lines of a paragraph never overlap. Messages, the line that introduces a teammate's message, the header and the composer grow with their text. The roster grows with its text too: each of its rows, headers and pinned bots is as tall as its lines need at the font size, and never shorter than at the default size, so at the largest sizes rows still have room between them; a name and a row's line end in an ellipsis rather than wrap. On the web the sizes are in rem and follow the browser's font size.

## Shape and space

- A 4 px spacing grid.
- Controls are 36 px tall on pointer devices and 44 px on touch screens; roster rows 56 and 64.
- Shapes are soft. Radii: 6 for badges, key caps, checkboxes and a bubble's tightened corner; 12 for fields, menu rows, chips of attached files and small tiles, such as a card's 28 px icon tile; 16 for roster rows, popovers, menus and cards; 20 for dialogs, bubbles and the cards in a conversation; 22 for the composer; 28 for the top corners of a sheet rising from a phone's bottom edge; and a pill for every button, switches and chips. The composer is a rounded rectangle at every height, never a pill, so text that wraps is never clipped by its corners.
- Consecutive bubbles from one speaker within five minutes form a group. On the speaker's side, the bottom corner of every bubble tightens to 6, and so does the top corner of each bubble after the first, so a group reads as one column with its tail at the end. A bubble on its own tightens only its bottom corner on the speaker's side. Bubbles in a group sit 4 px apart; a new group, or a card, starts 12 px below what came before. A pause of more than fifteen minutes puts a time between entries.
- Entry widths are layout tokens: a bubble takes up to 36 rem or 85% of the column, a card up to 36 rem or 92%, and the compact handoff and routine-change cards up to 28 rem. On a phone a card that asks the operator for something (a question, an approval, a credential) takes the full column. The composer grows with its text up to 15 rem, then scrolls.
- Borders are hairlines. Cards in a conversation and the composer sit on `raised`; popovers, menus, dialogs, sheets and the button back to a conversation's end, which floats over it, on `overlay`.

## Motion

Quick and springy: things overshoot a little and settle fast. Every move runs on one of three springs, each named by its natural frequency and damping ratio, so every platform draws the same motion from the same two numbers ([`springs.ts`](../../packages/design-tokens/src/springs.ts)):

| Spring | Frequency, damping | Settles in | Overshoot | For |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `pop` | 3.6 Hz, 0.5 | 571 ms | 15% | A press springing back, a menu or a short dialog opening, a switch's thumb, something arriving |
| `soft` | 2.3 Hz, 0.72 | 562 ms | 3% | Something moving into place: a dialog's panel rising |
| `slide` | 2.5 Hz, 0.86 | 392 ms | under 1% | A pane or a sheet sliding, a fold opening |

- **On the web** each spring is simulated once and written as a CSS `linear()` easing, sampled about 60 times a second, with the time it takes to settle as its duration: the utility `ease-pop` with `duration-(--duration-pop)`, and so on. **On Android** the same spring drives Reanimated as a physical spring of mass 1, stiffness ω² and damping 2ζω, so it has the same frequency and damping ratio.
- What has nothing to overshoot, a fade or a color, uses the `out` ease, `cubic-bezier(0.2, 0.8, 0.2, 1)`, over 150 ms for hover and press colors, 200 ms for a change of state, 260 ms for a status line swapping in place (`motion.swap`), and 300 ms for a scrim. Something leaving, such as a dialog closing, leaves on `out` in 200 ms, so a closed dialog never lingers.
- **Press:** a button goes down to 0.955 in 70 ms when pressed, a round icon button to 0.88 and a list row to 0.975, and springs back on `pop` when let go. Hover changes only the fill.
- **Dialogs and sheets:** on a phone a dialog that takes the screen rises from the bottom on `slide` and sinks back; from the tablet layout up it is a panel that rises a little and fades in on `soft`. A short dialog, such as a confirmation, and a menu pop in from slightly smaller on `pop`; on Android a menu does too, from its button's corner, over a scrim that fades. It lines up with its button's end edge, or with its start edge when the caller asks, as the composer's + does, and from a button in the lower half of the window it opens upward. It is placed by where its button is on screen, so beside a conversation, on a tablet or an unfolded phone, it stays by that button. Where the room past the button is short, as in a narrow roster, it narrows rather than run off the screen. On Android a pushed screen slides in over the one under it, which drifts back, and a form, Settings or a routine's runs rises from the bottom like a sheet; the system draws both.
- Avatars move continuously while they are on screen, on the budget in [Avatars](#avatars): only on-screen avatars step, list-size ones at half rate, and at most 24 at once. Their face eases into each pose; the body's hop and the antenna's lag behind it run on `pop`, and its lean on `soft`, so a bot settles as quickly as the chrome does. A kick is written as how far it carries the body, so it keeps its size on whichever spring carries it. A signal fades in over the state duration.
- **Panes and lines that open:** the details pane slides in on `slide` and out on `out` in 200 ms. It moves by a transform, never by its width, so the conversation beside it is laid out once, not on every frame, and the spring's overshoot never widens it. On the web the connection line unfolds above the app on `slide` and folds away the same way, keeping its last sentence while it goes; on Android it appears and goes in place.
- **Toasts** drop in from the top on `pop` and leave upward on `out` in 200 ms.
- **Loading** never jumps. What is still being read holds its place with skeleton rows the size of the rows they stand for, which appear only after 150 ms, so a quick read shows none, and then breathe slowly. Something that arrives in place of a skeleton or an empty space, such as an empty state's mark, pops in from slightly smaller on `pop`. Waiting with nothing to hold a place, such as connecting, shows three dots that pulse in turn. Connecting offers signing out, and in the desktop app another server, only once the wait passes eight seconds, so a load that is merely slow does not invite either; their place is kept from the start, so the dots above do not move when they arrive.
- **Organizing the roster:** a bot the operator pins, hides or deletes fades out of the rows on `out` in 200 ms as the rows under it close up on `slide`, and so does one moved into a folded section; one moved into an open section or out of one travels there as a reordered row does. One pinned pops into the pinned band on `pop` from 0.6, the band growing a line when it needs one, and one unpinned shrinks away from the band as the others make room on `slide` and its row arrives among the rows. Folding a section fades its rows out as the rows under it close up, and opening it fades them in as the rows under it make room, while its chevron turns a quarter on `pop`. A bot unhidden on the hidden screen fades away there as the rest close up. A row that only stops being listed, as after a search, goes at once, and nothing plays when the band first appears.
- **Roster reorder:** when the roster's order changes, usually because a bot spoke and rose to the top, rows travel to their new places instead of jumping. The order is the roster's own; the motion only draws the change ([`rosterMotion.ts`](../../packages/client-runtime/src/presentation/rosterMotion.ts)).
  - The rows that left the others' order (the shortest set whose removal leaves the rest in order; in a swap, the one that rose) lift: to 1.02 over an opaque ground with the `lift` shadow, above the rows they pass, and the row the latest move lifted above one still up from a move before. They travel on `slide` and are set down as they arrive. A row that rose makes its bot hop once.
  - The rows they pass step aside on `slide` 40 ms later. A row that only came or went appears or leaves in place, a new row fading in on `soft` as the others make room, and the rows it pushes along step at once. Everything else stays put.
  - A burst, several bots speaking within a second, retargets every row from where it is drawn: a row already travelling goes on toward its new place at once, and a row already lifted stays up without hopping again. Changes that land in one render are one move.
  - Nothing moves on the first rows drawn or when the operator's own search changes what is listed: results just appear.
  - The selected row stays selected, and nothing is announced: the rows move without a live region. On the web focus stays on the same row; on Android that has not been checked with TalkBack yet. Scrolled down, the first row in view that does not itself lift keeps its place, so rows moving above the view never push what the operator is looking at; at the top, the list stays at the top, where a row that rises comes into view.
  - **On the web** the list measures each row just before React moves it and plays the difference back as a transform (FLIP, on the Web Animations API), every row whose place on screen changed, the ones the list's own scroll moved included; the lift is CSS `scale` and an opacity. **On Android** every entry of the list has a height known before it is drawn, worked out from how tall a line of `text-base` and of `text-sm` is at the operator's font size, which the app reads once from a line of each drawn out of sight and again when the font size changes (at the default size, a row 64 px, a section's header 48 px and its empty line 40 px, the hidden row 56 px, a pinned bot 94 px and the band by its lines), so the list knows each one's place without measuring it and how far the row it keeps in place moved ([`keptPlaces`](../../packages/client-runtime/src/presentation/rosterMotion.ts), measured in px). A row that keeps its order but starts somewhere else, as under a band that grew, steps to its new place too. Each moved row's list cell carries its own Reanimated layout transition, which runs on the UI thread, and the list scrolls that far on `slide`, started by those transitions in the frame the rows start to move. The list keeps every row attached while off screen, so a row crossing the view from above it to below it is drawn on its way. The scroll and the rows' transitions reach the screen by separate paths, so they are not locked to the same frame: recorded on the Pixel 8 emulator, the row kept in place held within a pixel in some moves and shook by up to 18 dp for a quarter of a second in others. Under reduced motion the rows take their places at once, and a scrolled list still keeps its place.
- **In a conversation:**
  - Opening one, its entries fade up a few pixels into place, on `soft`.
  - The operator's message leaves the composer at once: it rises from the composer, shrunk, and pops into its place at the end on `pop`, while the send button's arrow lifts away on `out` and the composer settles back on `pop`.
  - A bot's message settles in on `soft`, at its full size from the start so nothing below it moves, and its text shows at a reading pace on `out`, longer for more (a third of a second to about a second).
  - Cards, notices and dates that arrive settle in the same way. What was there when the conversation opened appears in place, and so do files a saved draft already held; only what arrives while it is on screen moves.
  - At its end, the conversation stays pinned to the newest entry as entries arrive. Scrolled up, nothing moves it: messages others write are counted in a "New messages" pill over the end (cards, notices and times are not messages), and well away from the end a round button goes back to it. Both pop in and out on `pop`. Sending a message returns to the end.
  - A card that closes while on screen marks it: its actions fold away on `slide`, the outcome settles in on `soft`, an allowed approval draws its check and pulses once on `pop`, and a chosen option's check pops in on `pop`.
  - A turn that starts brings a small copy of its bot, 24 px and in the turn's state, into the timeline where the reply will appear, on `pop`. Beside it, one status line and the turn's clock. When the line changes, the old one leaves upward as the new one rises in, over 260 ms on `out` (`motion.swap`); the conversation header and the roster row change their line the same way, from the same state. The clock reads `m:ss`, then `h:mm:ss`, from the turn's start on the server, so a reload, a reconnection or another device shows the same time; it ticks once a second and not at all while the app is hidden or in the background.
  - A turn that ends folds its row in place into one quiet line, which pops in on `pop`, as tall as the row was; a reply the turn wrote comes in below it, so nothing above moves. A turn that has done no work keeps its row at the end, below anything the bot says meanwhile, until it ends: then the row goes and leaves the reply, or, for a turn stopped or failed before its first step, becomes a line saying so ("Stopped after 1m 4s"), with no steps to open.
  - A work fold's chevron turns on `pop` and its steps open and close on `slide`.
  - Something running carries a small live mark, a dot that breathes. It is stepped rather than drawn every frame, so a screen of busy bots does not hold the display at full refresh rate: it changes 8 times in 1.4 s.
- **Reduced motion** stops every move and every loop: nothing scales, slides or springs, entries appear in place, a status line changes in place, the live mark, the skeletons and the avatars stand still, colors still change, and each state keeps its still form. A turn under way keeps its still avatar and its clock.

The body is the first line the bot wrote when the operator allows previews ("Asks: …", "Needs approval: …" for cards, as in the roster, and "Scout: …" in a room), and otherwise what arrived: "Sent you a message", "Has a question for you", "Needs your approval", "Needs a credential", "Sent a teammate an urgent message". The icon is the still avatar, drawn in the theme the system is in as it shows, since the system draws the notification: the bot that wrote, in what the notification means to the operator (a question, an approval or a credential waits on them; a blocked bot shows blocked; anything else is done, not read yet, even while the turn goes on, since a reply's notification usually arrives before the roster hears the turn ended), or, for a room, its first two members' shapes as the roster shows them; the app's icon when there is no avatar to draw. A push on a phone shows the app's notification icon ([App icons](#app-icons)), since no image passes through a push, and on Android each kind shows in a channel of its own, which the system's settings list as Messages, Questions, Approvals, Credential requests and Urgent messages to teammates, so one kind can be silenced alone. A click opens the conversation or room, in a window that still follows the pairing that received it and whose roster still lists it (hidden bots included; a click before the roster loaded waits for it), and closes Settings, the details and any dialog over it; the profile in Settings keeps what was typed, and New bot holding a name, or the bot editor or memory viewer holding an edit, stays open over the conversation and keeps focus, so closing it lands there; signing out closes the ones that pairing received |
| Quiet conversation | What a conversation with nothing in it yet shows, centred: a bot's live avatar at 96 px, its name and what comes next (it speaks first, its hello waits for a model or a usage limit, or "Tell {name} what you need"); a room's two members, its name and who answers there, naming its host ("{host} answers what you write here, and an @ brings in any other member") |
| Notice | One quiet line where something stopped: a failed turn (with Try again), a reply that did not arrive, a chain of bots that hit its budget (a pause mark), a bot waiting in its own conversation, a bot that lost its working context, and a conversation that may be behind because J3 Bots could not apply what the engine reported. Notices are not announced; the presence line's changes of state are, by a live region outside the conversation |
| Connector tile | A connector's mark: its glyph, J3 Bots' own line drawing of what the service does and never a vendor's logo, in the text color on a `muted` tile of the 12 px radius, 36 px in a row and 48 px at the head of its page. A connector with no glyph, or one this client does not have, shows its first letter in the display face instead |
| Connectors screen | A dialog over Settings or a bot's details, the whole screen on a phone, that moves between its pages in place, each with Back. Its first page: the operator's connectors as rows (tile, name, a status word after a light in its tone, and who may use it), then the featured entries, then search results, and "Add your own" last. A status word is quiet when connected, `waiting` when it needs the operator and `blocked` when it fails; tool changes not yet answered add "Changes to review" |
| Connector page | Tile, name and publisher, then what is wrong and the one thing that fixes it (Sign in again, Unlock), any tool changes, who may use it (an "All bots" switch, then a box per bot, saved as it changes), the account, the origin or program in code type, the tools (five, then Show all) and Disconnect, which asks first. An entry's page holds its connect form instead: fields with their hints, a list to pick from for a field with choices, steps for an app of the operator's own, and the command line in code type for a command |
| Sign-in panel | Under the connector's status while a sign-in runs: that the browser has it, and until when. On the web served over plain HTTP, the steps to copy the address the browser ends on, a field for it and Finish, with a line that the desktop and phone apps skip the step. Open the page again and Cancel. A refusal says which and offers the one way on: Try again, or Unlock |
| Tool changes | A block on the connector's page, in a `waiting` frame: each new or changed tool with a New or Changed tag, a `waiting` tag when it takes different input, and what it said before. Accept (primary) and Not now. Once answered Not now it loses the frame and the button, and says bots won't use these tools until they are accepted |
| Connector suggestion | A card a bot writes: the connector's tile, its name and the bot's reason, with Connect (primary) and Not now. Once connected it says so; dismissed, it says that |
| Connector sign-in | A card a bot writes when its connector is signed out, with Sign in again; once signed in, or the connector removed, it says which and keeps no button |
| Routine change | A card in the timeline when a routine is created, changed, paused, resumed or deleted. Changes to a routine right after its creation or another change, with nothing between them, fold into that card; pausing, resuming and deleting keep cards of their own |

Cards in a conversation share one frame: the bubble's radius, a hairline border on `surface` and, in light, the faint `raised` shadow, an icon in a tile and a short title. A card that waits on the operator draws its border, tile and a "Waiting for you" tag in `waiting`, and its secondary actions (Deny, Decline, Dismiss) are outline buttons beside the primary one.

Question, approval and credential cards take a submitting state: while the operator's answer is on its way, their controls take no second answer but stay focusable (`aria-disabled` buttons, read-only fields), so focus stays where it was. Once answered, focus that was in the controls moves to the card, and the card is described by the line that says how it closed, so a screen reader announces the outcome with it.

## Words

Interface text follows the same rules as bot messages: plain, short, literal.

- Sentence case everywhere.
- A button says what it does to what: "Pause routine", not "Pause".
- Schedules are written out: "Weekdays at 8:47 AM", not a cron expression. An interval reads as one ("Every 15 minutes", "Every 6 hours at minute 00") only when it divides the hour or the day evenly; `*/7` minutes runs at :56 and again at :00, so it is not "every 7 minutes". A schedule with no plain sentence shows its expression. A schedule kept in another time zone names it, hourly ones included: "Every Monday and Thursday at 9:00 AM (Western European Time)", "Every hour at minute 15 (India Standard Time)". A single run gives its full date and time in the operator's time zone, and "Next run" is always in the operator's time zone.
- Times use the language's own short style ("8:47 AM" in English, "08:47" in Portuguese) and the operator's time zone. A time of day never breaks across lines: the space inside "8:47 AM" is a no-break space wherever a time appears, a full-width one, since the narrow one some runtimes use is set too thin at small sizes to read as a gap, in rosters, schedules, last runs and notices. A time standing alone reads "Yesterday at 1:40 PM"; inside a sentence it has its own form ("yesterday at 1:40 PM", "at 8:47 AM", "on Nov 13, 2026, 4:30 PM"), so no capital lands mid-sentence.
- Errors say what happened and what the operator can do.
- No exclamation marks, no mascot voice.

### Languages

Every interface sentence goes through the translation layer as a whole sentence with named placeholders. English is the source; Brazilian Portuguese is the first translation. A catalog that misses a key or drops a placeholder does not type-check. Plurals follow each language's rules. The language follows the browser unless the operator picks one in Settings, where each language is named in itself.

## Accessibility

- Text contrast of at least 4.5:1, and 3:1 for large text and interface marks, in both themes.
- Disabled controls are exempt from those minimums, as WCAG 1.4.3 exempts inactive components: a disabled control is drawn at half opacity, which is what marks it disabled, and it keeps its name for a screen reader.
- State is never carried by color alone: the avatar's form or a mark changes too.
- Every action is reachable by keyboard on desktop and by screen reader on every platform, with the same name it has on screen.
- Motion: everything honors the platform's reduced-motion setting (see [Motion](#motion)).
- Touch targets are at least 44 px. On touch screens a compact control grows to a real 44 px box, so controls that sit side by side or wrap onto a second line never cover one another; pointer devices keep the compact size. Menu rows, such as New, do the same.
- Light and dark follow the system unless the operator picks one in Settings, including a change the system made while the app was in the background. The choice is applied before the first paint: on the desktop the window opens with that theme's canvas as its background, and on Android 12 and later the splash takes it too. What the system draws for the app, its notifications and the desktop's tray, stays in the system's theme.
